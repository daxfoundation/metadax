# Learning in a second language -- working in a language that is not their first

Meta DAX guides > Situations > Learning in a second language

> **This is a situation, not a label.** Describe what you see and when it happens, not what the learner "is". If someone has mentioned a condition, that is for a professional to assess -- here we work only from what is observable.

## What you might see
- Understands the concept when explained in their first language but appears lost in the teaching language
- Pauses longer than expected before answering -- the thinking is there, the retrieval takes a moment
- Gives a correct answer in simpler words than the question was asked in
- Loses track of a task when instructions are long or multi-step
- Confuses words that look or sound similar across the two languages
- Switches languages mid-sentence when a word is on the tip of the tongue
- Performs noticeably better on tasks that use diagrams, numbers, or demonstrations than on text-heavy ones

## What's often going on
Working in a second language adds a translation layer to every instruction, question, and answer. That layer takes real cognitive effort -- effort that would otherwise go toward the learning itself. A longer pause before answering is not slow thinking; it is thorough thinking that includes a language step. Unfamiliar vocabulary in an instruction can make a task seem impossible even when the underlying knowledge is solid. The learner may understand a concept fully in their first language and struggle to show that understanding in the teaching language -- this is a language-load situation, not a capability gap.

## First moves
1. Separate language from content: check whether the learner understands the concept by letting them respond in any language, use diagrams, or gesture -- correct understanding in any form is still correct understanding.
2. Pre-teach the key words for each session before the content itself -- three to five subject-specific terms, in context, before anything else.
3. Speak in shorter sentences and pause between instructions -- long compound sentences multiply the translation load quickly.
4. Use diagrams, worked examples, and demonstrations alongside words whenever possible so understanding does not depend only on the teaching language.

## By guide type
- **Parent or caregiver:** If you share the learner's first language, use it freely to check understanding -- then invite the learner to try saying it in the teaching language too.
- **Private tutor:** Build a small running glossary of subject terms with the learner -- their own words in both languages, written in their handwriting.
- **Classroom teacher:** Give written instructions alongside spoken ones -- a learner can re-read a sentence; they cannot re-hear it.
- **Peer or older sibling:** Offer to explain something two ways: the easy everyday version first, then the more formal version -- that is good for both of you.
- **Coach or mentor:** Connect new vocabulary to things the learner already does or knows -- anchor new words to familiar experience.
- **Elder or grandparent:** Celebrate the learner's first language explicitly -- knowing more than one language is a genuine asset, not a complication.
- **Workplace trainer:** Provide written job aids in plain language alongside any verbal instruction -- a reference card reduces reliance on memory.
- **Volunteer teacher:** Allow think time without filling the silence -- a pause before an answer is normal and should not be prompted away.

## A 10-minute activity
**The two-column glossary**

1. (2 min) Identify the three or four words from today's topic that carry the most meaning -- the ones without which the task cannot be done. Write them in a list.
2. (3 min) For each word, ask the learner what they already know or guess it means. Write their words down, not the textbook definition. If they know the word in their first language, write that too.
3. (3 min) Together, build a simple sentence using each word in context -- a real situation, not an abstract definition. The learner writes the sentence in their own hand.
4. (2 min) The learner reads the sentences back. Keep the glossary page visible during the rest of the session and add to it over time.

## What not to do
- Do not interpret a slow response as not knowing -- wait a full and comfortable pause before offering help or moving on.
- Do not correct grammar or pronunciation during a content task -- mark the idea right when the idea is right; language form is a separate objective.
- Do not reduce the intellectual difficulty of tasks because of the language situation -- the goal is to reduce language load, not cognitive load.
- Do not treat the learner's first language as a problem to be eliminated -- it is a resource; using it to check understanding helps, not hinders, progress.

## What to watch, and record
- The learner explains a concept correctly in their first language or by gesture but not yet in the teaching language -> record as: "concept secure -- language form still developing in [topic area]"
- The learner uses a specific word incorrectly but consistently -> record as: "vocabulary target: [word] -- revisit in next glossary session"
- The learner works faster and more accurately on non-text tasks -> record as: "stronger without language load -- add more diagram/demonstration formats"
- The learner asks for time before answering and gives a correct answer -> record as: "processing time present -- do not prompt early"
