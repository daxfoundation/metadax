# Can Do It, But Can't Apply It -- fine on the drill, lost on the real-world problem

Meta DAX guides > Situations > Can Do It, But Can't Apply It

> **This is a situation, not a label.** Describe what you see and when it happens, not what the learner "is". If someone has mentioned a condition, that is for a professional to assess -- here we work only from what is observable.

## What you might see
- Scores well on practice exercises but freezes when the same skill appears in a
  different format or a real task
- Can repeat a procedure correctly but cannot say what it is for or when to use it
- Asks "is this the one where we...?" -- recognising the worksheet type rather than
  the underlying idea
- Applies a recently learned method to every new problem, even when it does not fit
- Gets confused when a word problem reorders the information compared to the drill
- Shows surprise or disbelief that the skill they just practised is the one needed now

## What's often going on
Drill practice builds a strong association between a skill and a very specific surface
form -- the layout, the wording, the workbook section. When the surface changes, the
cue that triggers recall disappears. This is a transfer gap, not a knowledge gap: the
learner has the skill but has not yet seen it in enough varied contexts to recognise
it without the familiar wrapper. A second possibility is that the learner memorised the
steps without a working model of why they work, so any unfamiliar framing derails them.

## First moves
1. After any drill, immediately present one "same skill, different clothes" problem --
   change the format, numbers, or real-world context. Name what you are doing: "same
   idea, new outfit."
2. Ask "where else could this come up?" before moving on. Let the learner generate
   contexts, however rough.
3. Vary practice contexts deliberately: oral, written, diagram-based, story-based,
   physical object. Use at least two in each session.
4. Work backwards: give the real-world answer and ask the learner to identify which
   skill produced it.

## By guide type
- **Parent or caregiver:** Point out the skill in everyday life when it arises
  naturally -- cooking, budgeting, directions -- without turning every moment into
  a lesson.
- **Private tutor:** End every session with a "disguised" problem drawn from a
  different subject or context; make it a game, not a test.
- **Classroom teacher:** Write the same learning objective in two formats on the board
  -- the drill version and a real-world version -- so learners see they are the same thing.
- **Peer or older sibling:** Swap problems: each person rewrites one drill question as a
  story problem for the other to solve.
- **Coach or mentor:** Connect the skill to a situation the learner actually cares about;
  let them see it working in their own domain first.
- **Elder or grandparent:** Share a story of when you used a similar idea without
  realising it was "school maths" or "school writing" -- normalise applied use.
- **Workplace trainer:** Present tasks in the order they appear at work, not in textbook
  order; the job context is the real context.
- **Volunteer teacher:** Use local, concrete examples the learner encounters daily;
  avoid abstract or foreign-feeling scenarios.

## A 10-minute activity
**Same Skill, Three Costumes**

1. (2 min) The learner solves one familiar drill problem to confirm the skill is there.
2. (3 min) Present the same underlying problem as a short story or real-world scenario.
   Do not label it. Let the learner try. If stuck, ask: "Does any part of this remind
   you of what we just did?"
3. (3 min) Present a third version -- a diagram, a physical object, or a spoken
   question. Again let the learner try first.
4. (2 min) The learner narrates what was the same across all three. Guide only if
   needed. Record the explanation in their own words.

## What not to do
- Do not assume that scoring well on drills means the concept is secure -- it means
  the drill is secure.
- Do not give hints that name the method ("try using long division here") -- that
  removes the recognition step the learner needs to practise.
- Do not add more drill repetitions when application fails; the problem is not quantity
  of practice, it is variety.
- Do not skip the "where else?" step because you are short on time -- it is the most
  important part.

## What to watch, and record
- Whether the learner can identify the right skill given a novel context -> record as:
  "unprompted transfer: yes / not yet on [topic]"
- Which context types work first (story, diagram, physical, oral) -> record as:
  "responds first to: [type]"
- Whether a single bridging question ("what does this remind you of?") is enough ->
  record as: "one prompt sufficient / needs more scaffolding"
- Whether learner-generated contexts show understanding or surface mimicry -> record
  as: "learner examples: on-concept / surface only"
