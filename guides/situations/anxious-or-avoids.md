# Anxious, or avoids starting -- freezes, stalls, or finds reasons not to begin

Meta DAX guides > Situations > Anxious, or avoids starting

> **This is a situation, not a label.** Describe what you see and when it happens, not what the learner "is". If someone has mentioned a condition, that is for a professional to assess -- here we work only from what is observable.

## What you might see
- Takes a long time to pick up a pencil or open a page when a task is set
- Asks many clarifying questions before doing anything, even when instructions are clear
- Says "I can't do this" or "I don't know where to start" before genuinely trying
- Finds reasons to leave the room, sharpen a pencil, or talk about something else
- Starts and immediately crosses out or erases before the guide can see the work
- Does fine in low-stakes conversation but stalls when a task is named or timed
- Watches others start rather than beginning independently

## What's often going on
Starting a task involves a moment of exposure -- the learner commits to an answer and it becomes possible to be wrong. For some learners, the cost of being wrong feels very high right now, whether because of past experiences with correction, a stretch of recent difficulty, or a sense that the task is bigger than they can manage. The stall is often protective: if you never start, you cannot fail. It can also reflect genuine uncertainty about where the entry point is -- an unclear first step looks the same from the outside as reluctance, and it is worth checking which it is before responding.

## First moves
1. Lower the entry cost -- say "just write one word, any word, even a wrong one" or "draw a quick sketch, it does not need to be right." The first mark on the page is the hardest.
2. Do the first move together, visibly and out loud, without it being the learner's turn yet. This normalises starting imperfectly.
3. Remove the evaluative frame temporarily -- "this is not marked, I just want to see how you think about it."
4. Break the task into the smallest possible first step and name only that step. Do not describe what comes after until that step is done.

## By guide type
- **Parent or caregiver:** Sit alongside and do your own task at the same time -- parallel working reduces the feeling of being watched.
- **Private tutor:** Start with something the learner can already do before introducing the harder task -- build momentum from a win.
- **Classroom teacher:** Offer a "private draft" option -- the learner can show you their first attempt before anyone else sees it.
- **Peer or older sibling:** Share a time you got stuck at the start yourself; make it ordinary rather than exceptional.
- **Coach or mentor:** Frame the first attempt as data collection, not performance -- "I want to see your first instinct, not a perfect answer."
- **Elder or grandparent:** Create a relaxed, unhurried atmosphere and avoid naming the task formally -- invite rather than instruct.
- **Workplace trainer:** Pair the learner with a colleague for the first attempt so the responsibility feels shared, not solo.
- **Volunteer teacher:** Keep session openings consistent and calm -- a predictable warm-up routine reduces start-up friction over time.

## A 10-minute activity
**The no-stakes first draft**

1. (2 min) Place the task in front of the learner. Ask them to read it silently. Tell them nothing will be handed in from this session.
2. (3 min) Ask the learner to talk through what they think the task is asking -- not to solve it, just to say what they notice. Respond only with "yes" or "what else?" -- no corrections yet.
3. (3 min) Ask the learner to write the first thing that comes to mind, even if it is a question or a wrong guess. Put your pen down so you are not writing at the same time.
4. (2 min) Reflect back what they produced positively and specifically: "You identified the key word here -- that is the right place to start."

## What not to do
- Do not push or count down -- time pressure makes starting harder, not easier, in this situation.
- Do not reassure with "it's easy" or "you know this" -- if the learner does not feel that way, it increases the gap between their experience and yours.
- Do not make the stall a topic of conversation in the moment -- noting it aloud ("why aren't you starting?") adds attention to the behaviour and rarely helps.
- Do not reduce the standard permanently -- the goal is to reduce the entry barrier, not to remove the learning challenge.

## What to watch, and record
- The learner makes a first mark or says a first word unprompted -> record as: "initiated independently after [what was in place]"
- The learner stalls only on a specific type of task -> record as: "avoidance pattern: [describe task type]"
- The learner starts readily in one context but not another -> record as: "starts well when [context] -- stalls when [context]"
- The learner crosses out or hides work before you see it -> record as: "self-conceals first attempt -- try private draft format"
