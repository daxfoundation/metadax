# Low Confidence After a Failure -- convinced they are "bad at it" and expects to fail

Meta DAX guides > Situations > Low Confidence After a Failure

> **This is a situation, not a label.** Describe what you see and when it happens, not what the learner "is". If someone has mentioned a condition, that is for a professional to assess -- here we work only from what is observable.

## What you might see
- Says "I can't do this" or "I'm just not good at [subject]" before trying
- Gives up quickly on new problems, especially ones that look like past failures
- Underestimates their score when asked to predict it
- Avoids attempting a problem if they are not sure they will be right
- Becomes visibly tense, quiet, or deflecting when the subject comes up
- Remembers failures vividly but dismisses successes ("that one was easy, it doesn't count")
- Effort drops: if they are going to fail anyway, why try hard?

## What's often going on
One or more visible failures have been attributed to a fixed personal quality ("I am
bad at this") rather than to changeable factors (not enough practice yet, a confusing
explanation, a bad day). This attribution becomes self-reinforcing: low effort produces
poor results, which confirms the belief. The learner may also have received feedback
that focused on the wrong answer rather than on what the work showed about their
thinking. Separating identity from performance -- and effort from outcome -- is the
core task here.

## First moves
1. Find a task the learner can do successfully right now and start there. An early win
   is not babying; it is evidence that challenges the "I can't" story.
2. When they succeed, name the effort or strategy specifically: "You re-read the
   question and that is what found it" -- not "you're so clever."
3. When they fail, model curiosity not comfort: "Interesting -- what happened there?"
   Treat errors as information, not verdicts.
4. Keep the first few sessions short and end on a success. The closing feeling matters.

## By guide type
- **Parent or caregiver:** Avoid praising talent or ability at home; praise the
  specific thing they did ("you kept going when it got hard").
- **Private tutor:** Begin each session with a quick problem you know they can do;
  it resets the emotional state before the harder work.
- **Classroom teacher:** Give feedback privately before returning graded work
  publicly; public failure in front of peers compounds the belief.
- **Peer or older sibling:** Share a time you struggled with the same thing and what
  changed -- a real example, not a pep talk.
- **Coach or mentor:** Reframe the goal as "one step further than last time" rather
  than a fixed standard, so progress is always visible.
- **Elder or grandparent:** Normalise struggle with a calm, unhurried presence;
  sitting alongside without judgment is often more powerful than advice.
- **Workplace trainer:** Pair early tasks with low-stakes stakes; make it safe to
  try and get it wrong before a real deadline.
- **Volunteer teacher:** Track and share small improvements across sessions so the
  learner can see their own trajectory in writing.

## A 10-minute activity
**Evidence Wall**

1. (2 min) Ask the learner to list three things in any subject they can now do that
   they could not do before. Accept any domain -- even outside school.
2. (3 min) Pick one skill from the current subject they have made any progress on, even
   small. Show them a piece of their own earlier work next to something more recent.
   Ask: "What changed?"
3. (3 min) Set one specific, small goal for the session -- not "understand fractions"
   but "correctly do two problems where the denominator is the same." Write it down.
4. (2 min) At the end, check the goal together. If met: record it. If not: "what would
   make it easier next time?" -- keep the frame on action, not ability.

## What not to do
- Do not say "you can do it, I believe in you" without pairing it with a concrete
  reason -- empty encouragement confirms they need rescuing.
- Do not protect the learner from all difficulty; avoiding hard tasks strengthens the
  belief that they cannot handle them.
- Do not over-explain why they should feel confident; feelings follow evidence, not
  argument.
- Do not make success contingent on a right answer -- reward engagement, re-attempts,
  and noticing their own error.

## What to watch, and record
- Whether the learner's self-prediction of score moves closer to actual over time ->
  record as: "predicted [X], scored [Y] on [date]"
- Whether early success in a session changes engagement later in the session -> record
  as: "warm-up win -> engagement: lifted / unchanged"
- Language the learner uses to explain errors -> record as: "error attributed to:
  self-fixed / external / effort-changeable"
- Whether the learner starts a session voluntarily vs. needing prompting -> record as:
  "session start: independent / prompted"
