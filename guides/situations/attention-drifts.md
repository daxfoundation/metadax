# Attention drifts -- starts well, then drifts off after a short while

Meta DAX guides > Situations > Attention drifts

> **This is a situation, not a label.** Describe what you see and when it happens, not what the learner "is". If someone has mentioned a condition, that is for a professional to assess -- here we work only from what is observable.

## What you might see
- Engages well for the first few minutes, then starts looking around, doodling, or going quiet
- Loses the thread of a task partway through and needs re-orienting
- Misses instructions given after the first minute or two of a session
- Picks up where they drifted rather than from where they stopped -- shows the drift was not deliberate
- Responds well when called back by name but drifts again within a short time
- Produces strong first sentences or first steps, then the quality drops mid-task
- Is not obviously tired -- can be alert and chatty but still drifts during structured work

## What's often going on
Attention tends to drift when a task runs longer than the learner's current comfortable focus window, when there is insufficient feedback or variation to keep the task feeling alive, or when the learner has lost the thread of what to do next and finds it easier to disengage than to ask. It can also happen when a learner is genuinely absorbed in a different thought -- not a distraction in the dismissive sense, but a competing mental thread that has grabbed hold. The environment matters too: a noisy or visually busy space shrinks the focus window considerably.

## First moves
1. Find the learner's current comfortable focus window -- notice when drift typically starts and plan tasks to fit inside that window with a natural break before it.
2. Build in a short, active re-engagement point partway through longer tasks -- a question to answer, a choice to make, or a quick physical action.
3. Reduce background noise and visual clutter if possible -- even a small change to the workspace can extend the window.
4. Give a clear, visible destination: "we are working until we reach step 3" -- knowing where the end is makes the middle more navigable.

## By guide type
- **Parent or caregiver:** Break home practice into short chunks with a physical movement between them -- even standing up and sitting down resets the focus.
- **Private tutor:** Plan sessions as a series of short tasks rather than one long one -- each task has its own clear endpoint.
- **Classroom teacher:** Use structured partner turns at natural intervals -- talking about the learning pulls drifting learners back into the material.
- **Peer or older sibling:** Check in by asking a question rather than a correction -- "where are you up to?" re-orients without shaming.
- **Coach or mentor:** Use goal-setting at the start of each chunk: "by the end of the next ten minutes, what will you have done?" -- ownership helps.
- **Elder or grandparent:** Allow a natural conversational rhythm -- a brief off-topic exchange can be a reset, not a derailment.
- **Workplace trainer:** Break demonstrations into segments with a task after each one -- passive observation is a fast route to drift.
- **Volunteer teacher:** Pre-plan a two-minute movement or discussion break at the point where drift typically starts; build it in rather than reacting to it.

## A 10-minute activity
**Short blocks with checkpoints**

1. (1 min) Set one small, specific task: "do only this one step -- nothing else yet." Write the step visibly where the learner can see it.
2. (3 min) The learner works. You stay nearby but do not interrupt unless they stop. Notice when drift starts without intervening yet.
3. (1 min) Stop the timer. Ask the learner to say one thing they have done and one thing that comes next. This is the checkpoint -- it is not a test.
4. (4 min) Repeat the cycle for the next step. At the end, ask the learner what they notice about how they work in short blocks. Record what they say.
5. (1 min) Agree together on how long a good block felt -- use that number to plan the next session.

## What not to do
- Do not call out the drift in front of others -- public correction makes the learner more self-conscious, not more focused.
- Do not assume drift means lack of interest -- a learner can be genuinely interested and still lose the thread after a few minutes.
- Do not extend sessions hoping focus will return on its own -- finishing a shorter, completed task is better than drifting through a long one.
- Do not use drift as a reason to reduce the intellectual level of the work -- the goal is to adjust timing and structure, not content difficulty.

## What to watch, and record
- The learner stays on task for a specific number of minutes before drifting -> record as: "focus window approx [X] min -- plan blocks to match"
- The learner re-engages immediately when asked a direct question -> record as: "re-engages on direct prompt -- use question-based check-ins"
- The learner produces stronger work in the first half of any block -> record as: "quality drops after [X] min -- shorten blocks or add midpoint break"
- The learner drifts less when there is a visible endpoint -> record as: "visible endpoint helps -- always show task boundary"
