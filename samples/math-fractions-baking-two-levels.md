# Fractions at the kitchen table, two ages at once — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*An illustrative example made with a simulated family — not a real family's record. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Priya · **Learners:** Sam (6) and Max (10) · **Age band:** 4–9 · **Subject:** Math

## Where the learner was stuck

Two children learn at the same kitchen table, and fractions caught both at different points. The younger, Sam, thought a bigger bottom number meant a bigger piece — asked whether a quarter or a half was more cake, Sam chose the quarter because "four is bigger than two." The older, Max, could name any fraction on sight and reduce it, but when a recipe needed a half cup plus a quarter cup, Max wrote three-quarters only by guessing, and could not say why. One wanted to see the pieces; the other needed the pieces to add up to something real.

## The approach (one activity, two levels)

The whole session is one batch of dough and one recipe, worked at two levels so neither child waits. Sam's level is cutting: fold and cut the dough into halves, then quarters, and line them up to feel that a half is the bigger piece even though "two" is the smaller number. Max's level is combining: the recipe calls for a half cup of one thing and a quarter cup of another, and Max's job is to say how much that is together and prove it by pouring. Same dough, same recipe, same table — the little one builds the pieces and the older one adds them.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Start with the dough, not the page. Sam cuts and arranges while Max reads the recipe aloud. Priya names one connection in plain words — "a half is one cut, a quarter is one more cut" — and then lets each child work their own level. The page comes out at the end: Sam answers which-piece-is-bigger questions by pointing, and Max answers add-these-two questions, each at their own pace with Priya between them. Keeping the two levels side by side, rather than running two separate lessons, is what lets one guide hold both children without the older one idling or the younger one lost.

## What only a person can do

Priya can see that Sam has stopped looking at the dough and started guessing, and move the dough back under Sam's hands before the guess hardens. She can tell that Max's "I don't get it" means boredom, not confusion, and hand Max the harder job of checking Sam's cuts. She knows which child needs to be first and which can wait a minute. A page gives the two levels; only Priya can read which child needs which one right now, and swap a job the moment the table tips from baking into testing.

## Where the learner leads

Both children lead something. Sam knows, better than anyone at the table, how to cut dough into even pieces so nobody complains their piece is smaller — that fairness is exactly what equal fractions mean, and Sam teaches it by doing. Max leads by teaching down: the hardest moment of the session asks Max to show Sam why a half and a half make a whole, using the pieces Sam just cut. Explaining it to a six-year-old is harder than adding the fractions alone, and it shows Priya what Max truly understands.

## The game on the learner's side

The game runs one activity at two levels. The first level shows two pieces and asks which is more cake — built to catch the "bigger bottom number" slip, so a wrong answer names it: "four pieces means each one is smaller." The second level gives two fractions from the recipe and asks for the total, with a picture of the measuring cups filling. It steps up after three correct in a row and steps back down after a miss, so each child settles at their own level without the guide tracking it. Nothing bad happens on a wrong answer; it just tells the table what to cut next.

## When to seek an evaluation

Most children sort out that a half is bigger than a quarter once they have cut enough real things, and most come to adding fractions slowly. But if a child, after many hands-on tries across a long stretch, still cannot hold the idea that the same whole can be split into different numbers of pieces, or seems unable to line up a number with an amount they can see and touch in a way that does not shift over time, it may be worth asking someone whose work is how children learn. A family doctor or an early-learning specialist can watch and tell you what they see. You do not need a label to have that conversation, and what you learn is yours to use as you choose.

## A way, never the way

Baking is one way in because this table already bakes together. Another pair of children might reach fractions through folding paper, sharing a pizza, or splitting a score — and another mix of ages might need the levels further apart or closer together. The point is not the dough. It is that the younger learner can see that a half is the bigger piece and the older can add the pieces into a whole. If your children reach for a different thing to cut, follow it.
