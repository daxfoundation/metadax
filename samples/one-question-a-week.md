# One question a week — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Marcus · **Learner:** Devi · **Age band:** 10–12 · **Subject:** Science

## Where the learner was stuck

Devi, who is eleven, can learn science facts when they are handed to her and give them back on a quiz. But somewhere along the way the wondering went quiet. She no longer asks why the kettle steams or why the apple slice goes brown; she waits to be told, and treats "science" as a pile of other people's answers to memorise. Marcus noticed it most on a walk, when she shrugged at something she would have pestered him about a year before. The gap is not that she cannot think — she thinks sharply when a thing is hers — it is that science has stopped being a question she gets to ask and become a lecture she sits through. A fact told is forgotten by the next week; a question she chased herself stays. What is missing is the asking.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Devi still notices odd little things — she just stops short of asking about them — and she loves being proved right. So the household builds a small weekly habit around her own noticing, and Marcus holds himself to one hard rule: he asks, he never lectures. On **Monday** one question goes on the fridge, written on a card — often one Devi half-wondered aloud, sometimes one Marcus offers if the week is quiet. She writes a **guess** underneath it, in pen, and signs it; the guess is allowed to be wrong, and being wrong on Monday is part of the fun of Friday. **Midweek** they do one small thing about it — a two-minute test at the kitchen counter, a look in the garden, or, if it truly cannot be tested at home, a quick look-up together — just enough to see what actually happens. On **Friday** Devi writes the **answer in two sentences, in her own words**, under her guess, and they compare the two. That is the whole week: notice, guess, check, say it back. Five minutes on Monday, a few midweek, five on Friday. The point is never the single fact; it is that she asked and chased it herself.

Three example weeks, all from the kitchen or the garden:

- **"Why does a cut apple go brown — and can we stop it?"** Guess on Monday. Midweek: three apple slices on a plate — one plain, one squeezed with lemon, one under water. Friday: she writes what she saw and why air is the culprit.
- **"Which dries faster on the line, a cotton shirt or a towel, and why?"** Guess. Midweek: hang both out, feel them each hour. Friday: two sentences on thickness and water held.
- **"Do the garden snails come out more after rain?"** Guess. Midweek: look at the same wall on a dry evening and a wet one. Friday: what she counted, and why damp suits a snail.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Hold the one rule above everything: you ask, you do not lecture. When Devi guesses wrong on Monday, say nothing to correct it — the wrong guess is what makes Friday worth reaching. Let the question be hers whenever you can; a question she half-asked on a walk, caught and written on the card, beats a better one you supply. Keep the midweek check genuinely small — one plate, one look, two minutes — because an experiment that needs a whole afternoon never happens twice. Prefer something testable at home; keep the look-up for the questions that truly cannot be seen. On Friday, make her write the answer in *her own words*, not copy a sentence from anywhere, and hold her to just two sentences — the squeeze is part of the thinking. When you compare guess and answer, celebrate a changed mind as the win: "you thought X, you found Y — that's exactly how it is meant to go." Never let it sprawl into a lesson. Stop on the two sentences, and let the next week's question start itself.

## What only a person can do

Marcus can catch the question Devi only half-asked — the shrug on the walk that a year ago would have been a "but why?" — and write it on the card so the week grows from her own wondering, which no curriculum printed in advance can do. He can decide a question is better tested than looked up, and the opposite, reading which one will keep her curious. He can keep his mouth shut when she guesses wrong, holding the surprise for Friday instead of flattening it with the answer on Monday. He can hear whether her two sentences are truly hers or borrowed, and ask one more "but why?" when they are borrowed. And he can model the thing itself — wondering out loud, being happily wrong, chasing it down — because science is caught from a person who does it, not delivered from one who recites it.

## Where the learner leads

Devi leads by choosing the question, more weeks than not. The noticing is already hers — she sees the odd little things; she had only stopped saying them aloud — and the whole habit is built to coax that noticing back into the open and treat it as the start of real science. Her guess leads too: it is her claim, signed in pen, and the week exists to test *her* idea, not Marcus's. His part is to name what she is doing — "that question you just asked, that's science, let's chase it" — and to keep the answer in her words, not his. Over the weeks the fridge fills with questions she thought of, and the asking, which had gone quiet, comes back as a habit.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is the week itself, run by the family at the fridge — no equipment but a card, a pen, and whatever is already in the kitchen or garden. Monday: a question goes up and the learner writes and signs a guess. Midweek: one small test or look to see what really happens. Friday: a two-sentence answer in the learner's own words, set beside the guess. Three levels set the reach: level one, the guide offers the question and the learner guesses and checks; level two, the learner supplies the question from something she noticed; level three, she also designs the small midweek test herself — what to compare, what to keep the same. It steps up after a run of weeks she drove herself and eases back if a week feels like a chore, because the wondering is the thing being protected. There is no losing: a guess proved wrong on Friday is the best kind of week, because something got learned the hard, memorable way. This is a habit a family keeps by hand at the fridge; it is not a screen to switch on. It runs well alongside [the science-words sample](science-words-that-stick.md), where the words from each week's answer earn a place on a running word wall.

## When to seek an evaluation

Going quiet on wondering for a stretch is ordinary — curiosity ebbs and flows, and for almost every child a gentle weekly habit that hands the asking back is enough to bring it out again, which is the whole point of this one. But if, over many months, a learner cannot form a guess about an everyday thing even with a friendly prompt, cannot say back in her own words something she saw with her own eyes a day before, cannot follow a two-step "notice, then check" from one day to the next, or grows sharply distressed whenever she is asked to think rather than recall and not with other work, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how children learn. A family doctor, or a learning specialist, can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later only gives you more options.

## A way, never the way

One-question-a-week is one way to turn science from facts-told back into questions-asked, chosen because this child notices things and likes being proved right. Another family would get there through a different door: a jar of questions drawn at random, a nature walk that must produce one "why," a single "wonder of the week" photographed and puzzled over. The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the fridge: one question, a guess written down before any checking, one small at-home test or look midweek, and a short answer in the learner's own words set against the guess — with the guide asking and never lecturing. The goal is that she wonders out loud again and learns to chase an answer herself. What you put on the card, and where you look for the answer, is yours to choose.
