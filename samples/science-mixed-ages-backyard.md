# One backyard science lesson for mixed ages — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Margot · **Learners:** Theo (8), Bea and Ines (both 5) · **Age band:** 4–9 · **Subject:** Science

## Where the guide was stuck

Margot had three children to teach at the same time: Theo, who reads fluently and finishes activities quickly, and Bea and Ines, who cannot yet read and lose focus within a few minutes. Every curriculum she tried was aimed at one grade, which meant Theo was bored while the little ones were lost, or the little ones were left behind while she worked with Theo. The real wrong answer the little ones gave when asked what plants need — "candy" — told her they were not yet connecting the question to anything real. She wanted one lesson that held all three without splitting her attention three ways.

## The approach (one lesson, three ages)

The lesson gives each child a different job based on what they can already do. Theo reads questions aloud and, later in the session, explains answers back to Bea and Ines in simple words. Bea and Ines always have something to touch, pour, or point at — their job is never to sit still and listen. The whole session runs in short bursts of two to three minutes of listening followed by a doing moment: pouring water on soil, lifting a rock, touching a leaf. The doing moments reset the little ones and give Theo a break from being the reader so he can observe and ask his own questions.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

The session opens outside, not at a table. Theo knows where the ants nest in the yard; Margot asks him to take everyone there and show Bea and Ines what he knows. This puts Theo in the guide seat from the start and gives the little ones something real to look at before any words are spoken. After the ant visit, everyone collects one thing: a bug, a leaf, a handful of soil. They hold them while Theo reads the first question aloud. The water experiment — pouring water onto soil and watching the colour change — is the moment that anchors the session for the little ones, because they can splash and pour freely. The session closes with a game on the page: Theo reads each question, Bea and Ines point at the screen to pick their answer, and the three levels let Theo try harder questions while the little ones stay with the doing parts.

## What only a person can do

Margot is the one who notices when Bea has gone somewhere else in her head and decides whether to bring her back or let a minute pass. She can tell the difference between Theo being bored and Theo being frustrated — those need different responses. She knows that Ines will stay with anything if she is allowed to get her hands wet, and that Bea sometimes needs a moment to herself before she can join in again. No page can carry that knowledge. The page gives a shape; Margot fills it with what she sees.

## Where the learner leads

Theo knows the ant nest better than anyone. Before the lesson content begins, he leads: he takes everyone to the nest, points out the trails, and tells the group what he has noticed about what the ants carry. This is not a warm-up exercise — it is the real start of the lesson, and Theo is the guide for it. Later in the session, the hardest game questions ask Theo to explain an answer back to a younger child in words they can understand. Explaining something simply to someone who does not yet know it is harder than answering a quiz question, and it tells Margot more about what Theo actually understands.

## The game on the learner's side

The game has three levels. The first level is pure doing: questions that require pouring, lifting, or touching something to answer. The second level is listen-and-pick: Theo reads the question aloud, Bea and Ines point at the answer on the screen. The third level is explain-it: the question asks the oldest to put an answer into simple words for a younger child. The game steps up after three correct answers in a row and steps back down after one miss, so it finds each child's level without Margot having to track it. Wrong answers are mapped to the specific mistake behind them, so the feedback is not just "wrong" but "here is what got mixed up."

## When to seek an evaluation

If, across several sessions, one child consistently cannot stay with even a short doing task — not distraction, but a pattern that does not shift when the task changes — that is worth watching. Similarly, if a child seems to have significant difficulty connecting words to the things they stand for, even after many hands-on experiences, or if a child's understanding seems noticeably further behind what you would expect compared to other children the same age in ways that do not improve over time, those are patterns that go beyond ordinary variation. You do not need a label or a diagnosis to ask for an outside view. A conversation with a family doctor, a child development centre, or an early learning specialist at a local school board can give you more information. Asking early is not an alarm — it is information, and you remain in charge of what to do with it.

## A way, never the way

This is one shape that worked for one parent's description of three children. Another family with the same ages and the same loves might need more time outside, or less reading, or a different anchor entirely. The oldest being the reader works here because Theo likes that job; if an older child does not, the whole shape shifts. Take what fits and leave the rest. The point is not to follow this plan but to find the version that fits your three.
