# Telling time with a real bus timetable (print-first) — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*An illustrative example made with a simulated family — not a real family's record. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Ruth · **Learner:** Jun · **Age band:** 7–9 · **Subject:** Math

## Where the learner was stuck

Jun, who is seven, can read the hour hand and say roughly what o'clock it is, but the minutes are a blur, and "how long until the bus comes" is a question Jun cannot yet work out at all. Asked how long from 2:10 until 2:40, Jun said "thirty o'clock," counting the numbers on the clock face rather than the minutes between them. Jun loves buses and already knows the timetable at the stop near home by heart — which bus, roughly when — but cannot connect that known thing to the hands on a clock.

## The approach (built through what they love)

The session is built on the one thing Jun already cares about: the real timetable at the local stop. Each question is a bus Jun knows — "it is 2:10, the bus leaves at 2:40, how long do you wait?" — so the minutes finally point at something real. The move is to count minutes in fives around the clock face, the way the timetable jumps in neat steps, rather than counting the printed numbers. Telling the time and working out how long until the bus become the same skill, anchored to a stop Jun can picture.

## For the guide: the shape of a session (print-first)

This one is built for paper, not a screen. Print the one page and fold it so only a single question shows at a time — Jun gets overwhelmed when a whole sheet of clocks appears at once, so the fold is doing real work. Jun answers the showing question, says the time or the wait out loud, and only then unfolds to the next. The guide keeps the answer key on a separate page, not on Jun's sheet, so Jun never sees the answers sitting beside the questions. Ruth sits beside Jun with the key, confirms or gently redirects, and lets Jun set the pace. Short is better than complete: a folded page of six questions done calmly beats a full sheet rushed.

## What only a person can do

Ruth can see the fold doing its job — or failing — and refold, cover, or set a question aside the moment Jun's face tightens. She can tell the difference between Jun guessing and Jun counting, and she holds the answer key so the page stays a set of questions rather than a test Jun can peek at. She can connect a question to a bus ride the two of them actually took last week, which no printed page can do. And she decides when six questions is plenty and it is time to walk to the real stop and watch a real bus come.

## Where the learner leads

Jun knows the local timetable better than Ruth does — which bus comes when, which one is always a little late. Before the page, Jun leads: Ruth asks Jun to walk her through the stop's schedule, and genuinely learns something. The hardest questions on the page flip it — Jun has to tell Ruth how long until the next bus, teaching the wait out loud — because explaining the gap is harder than reading a single time, and it shows Ruth what Jun has really grasped.

## The game on the learner's side

Because this is print-first, the "game" is the folded page itself: one question visible at a time, each a clock face and a bus from the timetable, getting one step harder as Jun gets a run right and easing back after a miss — the guide turns to an easier question on the key if Jun stalls. Early questions ask only the time shown; later ones ask the wait between two times, counting in fives. The guide's separate answer key names the likely slip beside each answer — counting the numbers instead of the minutes, or losing track at the top of the hour — so Ruth can name exactly what happened rather than only "not quite," and note where to start next time.

## When to seek an evaluation

Many seven-year-olds take a while with minutes and longer with elapsed time, and muddling the two is ordinary. But if, after many patient tries with real and concrete things over a long stretch, a child still cannot line up a number with an amount of time they can feel, or counting on consistently slips and resets in a way the child cannot notice or correct, it may be worth asking someone whose work is how children learn. A family doctor or an early-learning specialist can watch and tell you what they see. You do not need a label to have that conversation, and what you learn is yours to use however you choose.

## A way, never the way

A bus timetable is the way in here only because this child already loves the buses. Another child would find the minutes through a favourite show's start time, a sports clock, or an egg timer in the kitchen — and another child might do better on a screen than on paper, where this one needed the fold. Keep what works: one question at a time, the answers kept apart, the pace set by the learner. The goal is that the child can read the minutes and work out the wait. The thing you hang it on is yours to choose.
