# The blank-page frame — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Rosa · **Learner:** Felix · **Age band:** 10–12 · **Subject:** Writing

## Where the learner was stuck

Felix, who is eleven, is a careful, capable worker who freezes the moment a task is open-ended. Hand him a worksheet with blanks to fill and he is fast and accurate; ask him to "write about anything you like" or "write a paragraph about the book" and he sits, pen up, writing nothing for the whole session. It is not that he has no ideas — talk to him and they pour out — it is that an open page offers too many choices at once and he will not commit to one in case it is the wrong one, or not good enough. The blank page is not too hard for him; it is too wide, and his care for getting things exactly right has hardened into a wall he cannot start over.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Felix loves getting things exactly right and building from clear step-by-step instructions — a model kit with every piece numbered is his idea of a good afternoon — and the approach gives writing the same scaffolding. It removes the choices. Instead of a blank page, Rosa gives him a topic-sentence frame with three "because" slots: "____ is the best ____, because ____, because ____, and because ____." They fill every slot aloud together before a single word is written, so the committing happens in speech, where he is fluent, not on the page, where he freezes. Once the frame is full, the "writing" is just copying down what he already said. The next step keeps the rails on: he takes a short model paragraph and rewrites it changing exactly one thing — one noun, one opinion — then two things, growing from the model a little at a time rather than facing an empty page. Five minutes, and they stop on a finished sentence.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Never open with a blank page. Start with Felix leading — he explains the order of steps in something he built, which is him being fluent and exact, the very strengths the session will lean on. Then put the frame in front of him and fill it aloud first; do not let him near the writing until every slot has been said out loud, because the freeze lives in the choosing, and choosing out loud with Rosa is safe. When it is time to write, offer to take dictation for the first draft — he says it, she writes it — so that getting words down and forming them by hand stay two separate jobs and neither jams the other. Keep the one-change rewrite small on purpose: one change is a decision he can make without it feeling like the whole thing rests on him. Stop on a finished, correct sentence — for a perfectionist, ending on something right is what makes him willing to start again tomorrow.

## What only a person can do

Rosa can tell the difference between Felix having no idea and Felix having too many and refusing to pick — the same blank page, two different problems, and only someone talking with him can tell which. She can take dictation at the exact moment the handwriting is what is jamming him, separating the composing from the writing so he can do one at a time. She can hold the standard where he cannot: telling him that a first draft is allowed to be ordinary and that they will make it better afterwards, which is a thing a perfectionist often cannot give himself. And she can decide to stop on one good sentence rather than push for a whole paragraph, because for this child a clean ending is what unlocks the next start. A page cannot lower a wall it cannot see.

## Where the learner leads

Felix knows, better than Rosa, how to follow and explain a sequence of exact steps — the thing he does when he builds from instructions. Before any writing, he leads: he walks her through how something is put together, in order, leaving nothing out. That is the skill the frame borrows: a paragraph is steps in an order — claim, because, because, because — followed one at a time, exactly the way he follows a build. Rosa's part is to name it: the careful, in-order way he explains his model is the way the two of them will build a paragraph, one slot at a time. He supplies the method; she points out that writing is just another thing built in steps.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is one short round the parent runs on paper, and it never shows a blank space. It gives a topic-sentence frame with three because-slots and a small bank of filled-in options to choose from, so every move is picking, not generating from nothing. First Felix picks what the sentence is about, then chooses or says each "because" aloud, and only then writes the full sentence down — or has it taken down for him. Three levels loosen the rails a step at a time: level one is choosing from options to fill the frame; level two is rewriting a model sentence with one change of his own; level three is two changes, then three, until the frame is doing less and he is doing more. It steps up after he finishes a round without stalling and eases back the moment he freezes, so the page is always narrow enough to start on. Nothing is ever blank and nothing is ever wrong for being plain — a finished, ordinary sentence is the whole win.

## When to seek an evaluation

Freezing at an open-ended task while sailing through a structured one is common, especially for a careful child who wants to get it right, and for most the frame-first, fill-aloud approach is enough to get writing moving — which is the point of this session. But if, over many months and across many subjects, getting any words onto the page stays far harder than how fluently the child speaks, if the hand itself freezes or tires in a way that does not ease with practice, or if the distress at starting is sharp and persistent rather than ordinary reluctance, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how young people learn. A family doctor or a learning specialist can observe, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later only gives you more options.

## A way, never the way

A topic-sentence frame with three because-slots is one way to make an open task startable, chosen because this child trusts clear steps and freezes without them. Another child would need a spoken sentence caught and written down, a picture to label, or three options to choose between. The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the frame: remove the choices, fill the frame aloud before writing anything, take dictation so composing and handwriting stay separate, and grow by one small change at a time from a model. The teen version of the same freeze — fluent out loud, stalled on the page — is [turning a hockey argument into a paragraph](writing-argument-hockey-session-two.md). The goal is that he can start. The rails you give him to start on are yours to choose.
