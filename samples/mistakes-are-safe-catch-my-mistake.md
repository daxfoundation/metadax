# Mistakes are safe: catch my mistake — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Mara · **Learner:** Theo · **Age band:** 4–6 · **Subject:** Math

## Where the learner was stuck

Theo, who is four, is quick and willing right up until the moment something comes out wrong. He miscounts a row of buttons, or a puzzle piece will not go where he thought, or he writes a letter that does not look like the one on the card — and his face falls, he says "I can't do it," and he gets up and walks away. It happens the same way whatever the work is: counting, a puzzle, a letter. He is not refusing to learn and he is not short on ability; the trouble is that being wrong feels like a small disaster, and so the first mistake ends the session before any learning can stand up again. Longer sessions and gentle "try again"s had made it worse, because they kept him sitting at the exact spot where the bad feeling lives. The gap is not his thinking — it is that a mistake is not yet a safe place to be.

## The approach (built through what they love)

The one thing Theo cannot resist is catching a grown-up being wrong — the delight of being the one who knows better. So Mara hands him that on purpose. First they do a thing together, her hands alongside his, so a right answer is already in the room and nobody is being tested. Then she plays it wrong: she counts three buttons "one, two, three, four," slowly, straight-faced, and waits. Theo lights up — "No! That's three!" — and counts it right himself, laughing, because now the mistake is hers and catching it is the whole fun. Being wrong has quietly changed its meaning: it is the thing you look for and pounce on, not the thing that ends the game. She keeps it to five or ten minutes and stops while he is still winning, so the session's last feeling is the good one. Every time he tries — even a try that misses — she names it out loud: "you had a go at that, that's the brave bit." If he does walk off, she lets him, and comes back later with an easier version, so the next start is one he can win.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Keep it short — five to ten minutes, most days — and end it on purpose while he is ahead, not when he tires. Start together: do one round hand-in-hand so a correct version exists before anyone is on the spot. Then switch to catch-my-mistake, where Mara is the one who gets things wrong and Theo is the catcher. Make the mistakes obvious at first — count a clear three as four — and only later make them subtler. Praise the trying, out loud and specifically: not "good boy," but "you kept going even when it was tricky." If he gets one wrong himself, treat it exactly as he treats hers — "oh, catch me out, let's count it again" — so his mistake and hers are the same friendly thing. If he walks away, let him go without a fuss; the walking away is information, not defiance. Come back later the same day with a version one notch easier, so the door he reopens is one he can get through.

## What only a person can do

Mara can read the exact half-second when Theo's face starts to fall and head it off with a deliberate mistake of her own, turning the moment away from collapse before it arrives. She can judge how wrong to be — obvious enough to catch today, subtle enough to stretch him tomorrow. She can tell the difference between a walk-away that means "I am done" and one that means "make it easier," and answer each differently. She can decide that four happy minutes beats ten that end in tears, and stop on the laugh. And she can hold, steadily and warmly, the one thing the whole approach rests on: that being wrong is safe here. A worksheet cannot be caught out, cannot laugh with him, and cannot choose to end on a win.

## Where the learner leads

Theo leads the catching. Nobody taught him to delight in spotting a wrong count — that is his, already, and it is exactly the attention a mistake needs: looking closely, checking, noticing when something is off. Mara's part is to point out what it is for. The sharp eye he turns on her counting is the same eye he can turn on his own, gently, once being wrong stops stinging. He supplies the pounce; she turns it, over time, from catching her into checking himself — not as a test he passes or fails, but as the fun of finding the slip and fixing it. The lead is his; she only shows him where it points.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is "Catch my mistake," one short round a parent runs at the table with anything there is a handful of — buttons, blocks, grapes, counters. The grown-up counts a small group out loud and gets it wrong on purpose — says "four" for three, skips a number, counts one button twice — and the child catches it and counts it the right way himself. Three levels set the challenge: level one is an obvious miss in a group of three or four; level two is a trickier miss — a double-count or a skipped number — in a group up to six; level three lets the child take a turn making the mistake for the grown-up to catch. It steps up after a run of happy catches and eases back the moment the fun dips, because the fun is the point and the counting rides along underneath it. There is no wrong move in this game — a missed catch just means the grown-up gets away with it that round and tries an easier one next. This is a game a parent plays by hand; it is not a screen to switch on.

## When to seek an evaluation

Getting upset at being wrong, and needing mistakes made safe before learning can go on, is an ordinary and very common stage for a young child — and for most, short playful sessions that turn being wrong into a game are enough to loosen it, which is the whole point of this one. But if, over many months and across many kinds of gentle, low-pressure play, a child still comes apart at the smallest error, cannot bear to try anything he is not already sure of, stays distressed long after a mistake rather than recovering with a laugh, or shows the same sharp upset around other everyday changes and not only around being wrong, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how young children learn and feel. A family doctor, or an early-childhood or early-learning specialist, can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later only gives you more options.

## A way, never the way

Catch-my-mistake is one way to make being wrong safe, chosen because this child already loves catching a grown-up out. Another child would need a different door: a puppet who gets things gloriously wrong, a "whoops, let's fix it" song, a tally where every mistake-caught earns a point for the team you are both on. The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the buttons: do it together first, let the guide be the one who is wrong so being wrong turns funny, praise the trying out loud, keep it to five or ten minutes, stop while he is winning, and when he walks off let him and return later one notch easier. The goal is that a mistake becomes a safe, even funny, place to be — so that trying can carry on. What you count, and who gets caught, is yours to choose.
