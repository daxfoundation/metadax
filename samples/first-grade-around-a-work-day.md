# First grade around a work day — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Priya · **Learner:** Jonah · **Age band:** 4–6 · **Subject:** Reading

## Where the learner was stuck

Priya works from home and wanted to start Jonah, who is six, on learning at home. Two things were tangled. The first was hers: between calls and messages she could not picture where lessons would actually go, or how many hours a day it should take, and every plan she sketched assumed a school-shaped day she did not have. The second was Jonah's: when a lesson ran long he drifted — eyes to the window, sliding off the chair, a sudden need for water — not because the work was too hard but because it went on past the point a six-year-old can hold. The stuck point was the shape of the day, not the content: Priya could not see how the lessons fit around her work, and Jonah could not stay with a lesson that outlasted his attention.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Jonah loves trains, and more than the trains he loves the order of them — which car comes after which, the whole line laid out so you know what is coming. Priya built the day to look like that. The guided part is about ninety minutes in total, but never in one block: it is cut into short cars the length a six-year-old can ride. Reading is the longest car at about twenty minutes, maths about fifteen, writing about ten, strung through the day in two or three sittings that sit in the gaps around Priya's calls. Each car is a picture on one schedule taped where Jonah can reach it — a book for reading, counters for maths, a pencil for writing, and just as real: a car for play, a car for a chore, a car for outside, and a read-aloud car at the end. Jonah ticks each car as the day rolls through it, so he can always see what is now and what is next. The rest of the day — most of it — is play, chores, outdoor time, and being read to. Ninety minutes of guided work, predictably placed, is the engine; the long free stretches are the rest of the train.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Each car is short on purpose. Start the reading car first, when attention is freshest, and keep it near twenty minutes; stop it while Jonah is still with you, not when he has drifted. Maths and writing are shorter still. Put the cars in the gaps your work leaves — one before your first call, one after, one in the afternoon — and keep them in the same order most days so Jonah can predict them. Five minutes into a car that is going well is not a reason to add ten more; stop on a win, tick the car, and let the next free stretch be the reward. If a call runs over, the car waits; the schedule bends around the work rather than breaking. The ninety minutes does not have to be exact or unbroken — it is a rough shape, not a quota — and some days two cars is plenty. What matters is that Jonah can see the line of the day and feel each car end before he tires of it.

## What only a person can do

Priya can see the exact moment Jonah starts to drift and end the car a minute early, so the reading block finishes on a good line rather than a lost one. She can slide a car later when a call runs over without the whole day falling apart, and she can judge, watching him, whether today is a three-car day or a two-car one. She can tell restlessness that means "I am done" from restlessness that means "I need to move first," and send him outside before the next car rather than fighting through it. And she can hold the thing the schedule is really for — that the day is predictable and the work ends before it sours — in a way no fixed timetable printed once could. A plan on paper cannot watch a child's attention; a parent can.

## Where the learner leads

Jonah leads the order. He is the one who knows how a train should be made up, and Priya lets him build the day's line each morning — choosing which car comes after which among the few that have to happen, and setting the play and outside cars where he wants them. That ordering is not a frill; deciding what comes next, and seeing the sequence through, is real thinking, and it is already his. Priya's part is to name what it is for: the care he gives to making up the train is the same care that gets the reading car and the maths car done, because he can see them coming and knows the free car is just down the line. He supplies the order; she points out that the order is what makes the day work.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is "build the day's train," played by hand at the start of the morning with a small set of picture cards — one for each car: reading, maths, writing, play, a chore, outside, read-aloud. Jonah lays them out in order on the table or along the floor, and ticks or flips each card as that part of the day is done, so the schedule is something he makes and runs, not something done to him. Three levels set how much he leads: level one, Priya lays the must-do cars and Jonah adds the play and outside cars and ticks them off; level two, Jonah orders the whole line himself with Priya checking; level three, Priya slips one card into the wrong place on purpose and Jonah catches it and fixes the order. It steps up as building the train becomes easy and eases back on a scattered morning. There is no losing — a card in the wrong spot just gets moved. This is a game played with paper cards by hand; it is not a screen to switch on.

## When to seek an evaluation

A six-year-old who can only stay with a lesson for a short stretch is doing exactly what six-year-olds do, and short predictable blocks are usually all it takes for the drifting to settle as attention grows. But some patterns are worth describing plainly to someone whose work is how young children learn. If, over many months of short, gentle, well-placed blocks, a child still cannot settle to even a few minutes of anything, or seems not to hear or follow simple spoken instructions, or the restlessness is sharp and constant across play as much as lessons and not easing with age, it can be worth a conversation. Your family doctor or an early-learning specialist can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier only gives you more to work with.

## A way, never the way

A train of cars is one way to shape a short guided day around a parent's work, chosen because this child already loves the order of things. Another family would picture stepping stones across the morning, a row of boxes to tick, or simply three short "chapters" with long play between them. The parts worth keeping are not the trains: guided work kept to roughly ninety minutes of short, predictable blocks — reading about twenty, maths about fifteen, writing about ten — placed in the gaps around the work, one visible schedule the learner can see and tick, the rest of the day given to play and outside and a read-aloud, and every block stopped on a win before it sours. This is one family's way of fitting it around a working day, not the way, and it makes no claim about what any law or place requires of you. How much a day it takes, and how you lay the cars out, is yours to choose.
