# Grammar from your own book — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Rosa · **Learner:** Milo · **Age band:** 7–9 · **Subject:** Writing

## Where the learner was stuck

Milo, who is eight, can fill in a grammar worksheet — circle the verb, underline the noun — and often gets it right. But close the worksheet and open the adventure book he reads at bedtime, and the grammar vanishes: he cannot find the verb in a sentence he just read and loved, and nothing from the worksheet shows up in his own writing. The rule lives in one box and his reading in another, and the two never meet. Rosa had a workbook on the shelf and a nagging sense it was not working, and it was not — not because Milo cannot learn grammar, but because grammar taught on strangers' sentences stays stuck to strangers' sentences. The gap is not his ability — he knows the story better than she does — it is that the grammar never touched a sentence he cared about. What is missing is the bridge from the rule to the book in his hands.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Milo loves his adventure book and loves being the expert on its story, so the grammar comes straight out of it — tonight's chapter, not a workbook. Each session, Rosa and Milo pick **three sentences** from the pages he is already reading, and they do **one move** with them — only one, the same move across all three, never a grab-bag. The moves rotate across sessions:

- **Find the doer and the doing.** In "The dragon circled the tower," who is doing something (the dragon) and what are they doing (circled)? Three sentences from the chapter, same question each time. This is subject and verb, named in plain words first.
- **Join two short sentences.** Find two short sentences near each other — "It was dark. Milo lit the lamp." — and join them with *and*, *but*, *so*, or *because*, then read both versions aloud to hear which the author might have picked.
- **Change the time of a sentence.** Take one sentence and move it from now to yesterday, or yesterday to now — "The dragon circles the tower" / "circled" — hearing how the doing-word changes.

Always, the **last sentence is Milo's own**: after three from the book, he writes one sentence doing the same move, about the story or about his day. Grammar words like *verb* and *conjunction* can come in once the plain version is solid, laid over something he already understands. Five or ten minutes, and they stop on a sentence he got. No workbook anywhere.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Use the book he is actually reading, and pick the three sentences from the pages he read most recently, so the grammar lands on words still warm in his head. Do one move per session and only one — the point is depth on a single idea, not a tour of five. Lead with plain language before the technical word: "the doer and the doing" before "subject and verb," "joining words" before "conjunctions," so the idea is understood before it is labelled. For joining and for changing time, always read both versions *aloud* — the ear is doing half the teaching, hearing why a writer chose one shape over another. Keep the three book-sentences short and clear; skip a sentence that is too tangled today. End every session with Milo's *own* sentence doing the same move, because using it himself is where the rule finally crosses the bridge into his writing. Praise the finding and the having-a-go, not neat handwriting. Keep it to five or ten minutes and stop on a sentence he got right, even if there was room for one more.

## What only a person can do

Rosa can open the very book Milo is reading tonight and pull the grammar from a sentence he already cares about — a worksheet can only offer sentences chosen in advance for no child in particular. She can hear whether "find the doer" has clicked or whether he is guessing, and ask the one more question that settles it. She can skip the sentence that is too knotted for today and pick a cleaner one from the same page, a judgement no printed exercise can make. She can read both the joined and unjoined versions aloud and let Milo *hear* which the author chose, then follow his ear into why. And she can decide that one good sentence of his own is the win and stop there, so grammar stays tied to a book he loves rather than a chore he dreads. A workbook cannot reach for the book in the child's hands.

## Where the learner leads

Milo leads with the book. He is the expert on its story — he knows what the dragon did and why — and the session starts from that authority, not from a rule handed down. He often picks the three sentences himself, choosing bits he liked, and a sentence he chose is a sentence he will look at closely. His own sentence at the end leads too: it is his, about his story or his day, and it is where the grammar stops being Rosa's and becomes his. Her part is to name what he is already doing — you found who was doing the thing; that doer has a name, we call it the subject — so the labels land on understanding he already had. He supplies the book, the sentences, and the expertise; she supplies the one small move and the bridge from the page to his own writing.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is one short round a parent runs with the book the child is reading, a pencil, and a scrap of paper — nothing else. Pick three sentences from tonight's chapter and do one move with all three: find the doer and the doing, join two short ones, or change the time; then the child writes one of his own. Three levels set the reach: level one, the guide points to the sentences and names the move in plain words; level two, the child picks the three sentences and tries the move himself; level three, the child names the move using the proper word — subject and verb, joining word, past and present — and writes two of his own. It steps up after a run of sentences he handled easily and eases back the moment a move gets muddled, so the grammar stays close to what he can do. There is no losing: a wrong guess just means reading the sentence aloud together and trying again, slower. This is a game played by hand with a real book; it is not a screen to switch on. It pairs well with [the decodables-to-real-books sample](decodables-to-real-books-take-turns.md), where the same real book is the thing a reluctant reader is coaxed into on his own.

## When to seek an evaluation

A gap between doing a grammar worksheet and using grammar in real reading and writing is very common at this age — and for nearly every child, pulling the grammar out of a book they love is enough to close it, which is the whole point of this one. But if, over many months of short, gentle sessions, a child still cannot find the doer and the doing in a simple sentence he has just read, cannot hear the difference when two sentences are joined or a sentence's time is changed even when you read both aloud, cannot carry a single move into a sentence of his own, or grows sharply distressed whenever sentences and their parts come up and not with other work, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how children learn. A family doctor, or a literacy or language specialist, can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later only gives you more options.

## A way, never the way

Pulling three sentences from tonight's chapter is one way to make grammar stick, chosen because this child loves his book and likes being its expert. Another child would get there through a different door: sentences from a comic, the words of a favourite song, a recipe, or the back of a cereal box. The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the adventure book: take the grammar from something the child is already reading and cares about, do one move per session — doer and doing, joining two sentences, changing the time — read the changes aloud so the ear teaches, and finish with the child's own sentence. The goal is that grammar crosses the bridge from a rule into the sentences he reads and the ones he writes. What book you open to find it is his to choose.
