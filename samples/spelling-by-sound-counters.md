# Spelling by sound with counters — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Priya · **Learner:** Jonah · **Age band:** 4–6 · **Subject:** Writing

## Where the learner was stuck

Jonah, who is five, can read a handful of words and loves to. But when Priya asks him to write a word — even one he has just read — he writes a long string of letters that have little to do with its sounds: "bdjxid" for bridge, "kprln" for garden. He is not being careless; he is writing down the letters he can think of, because a word still arrives in his ear as one lump of sound rather than a row of separate sounds he could match to letters. Spelling lists and copying had not shifted this, because they ask him to remember letters before he can hear what the letters are for. The gap is not his memory and not his effort — it is that he cannot yet pull a spoken word apart into the sounds that make it.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Jonah likes lining up and sorting small things — buttons, bottle tops, a handful of counters — and a counter is a perfect stand-in for a single sound. So before any letter is written, Priya says a short word slowly and Jonah pushes one counter forward for each sound he hears: c–a–t is three counters, pushed one at a time. Only then does he write one letter above each counter. This runs the opposite way to [the blending sample](reading-blending-dinosaurs.md), where separate sounds are pushed together into a word; here a whole word is pulled apart into its separate sounds. They start with CVC words — cat, sun, map — where every sound is a single letter. Digraphs come next: sh, ch and th each get one counter, because each is a single sound even though it takes two letters, so "ship" is three counters and "chop" is three. Five minutes a day, and they stop on a word he got.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Keep it to five minutes, most days. Start with Jonah leading — he sorts the counters into colours or lines them up his own way, and Priya follows his rule — so the counters are his before they are the lesson's. Then one short word at a time: say it slowly, let Jonah push a counter per sound, and only write once the sounds are counted. Treat a phonetic spelling as a success, not an error: if he writes "treet" for treat or "nite" for night, he has heard every sound and matched a reasonable letter to each, which is exactly the skill being built — say so out loud, then show the grown-up spelling alongside without rubbing his out. Exception words that cannot be sounded out come separately, only two at a time, and are learned by sight rather than forced onto the counters. Stop on a word he got right, even if the five minutes could have held one more.

## What only a person can do

Priya can hear the difference between Jonah missing a sound and Jonah hearing it but not knowing its letter — the same wrong spelling on the page, two completely different things, and only a listener beside him can tell which. She can slow her own voice on the exact word where his counters stall, stretching caaat until the middle sound surfaces. She can decide that "treet" is a win worth stopping on rather than a mistake worth correcting today. And she can tell when a tired guess means the session is over, and end it there, on a good one, so tomorrow starts from willingness. A page cannot stretch a sound in response to the child in front of it.

## Where the learner leads

Jonah leads the counters. He lines them up and sorts them better than Priya does, and she lets him set them out his way before any word is said. That lining-up — one thing, then the next, in a row — is the very move that spelling needs: a word is sounds in a row, pushed out one at a time, just like his counters. Priya's part is to name it: the careful way he lays the counters down is the careful way the two of them will lay a word down, sound by sound. He supplies the habit; she points out what it is for.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is one short round the parent runs at the table with a handful of counters. Priya says a word; Jonah pushes one counter for each sound, then writes a letter above each counter. Three levels set the words: level one is three-sound CVC words where every sound is one letter; level two brings in digraphs — sh, ch, th — each as a single counter; level three mixes them and lets a word run to four sounds. It steps up after a streak of words he spells sound-for-sound and eases back after a miss, so the words stay at the edge of what he can hear. A phonetic spelling counts as correct for the game, because every sound is there; the guide key quietly notes which one it was, so Priya knows which grown-up spellings to show next. Exception words are kept out of the levels and practised separately, two at a time. Nothing bad happens on a miss; it just tells the two of them which sound to listen for tomorrow.

## When to seek an evaluation

Hearing the separate sounds in a word is a skill that grows with exactly this kind of short, playful practice, and spelling a word the way it sounds is a normal, healthy stage on the road to standard spelling — not a problem to fix in a hurry. But if, after a long stretch of patient five-minute sessions, a child still cannot hear or push out the separate sounds in a simple word, cannot tell where one sound ends and the next begins even when you stretch the word right out, or tires sharply whenever sounds and letters are involved and not with other work, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how children learn. A family doctor or an early-learning or reading specialist can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later only gives you more options.

## A way, never the way

Counters are one picture of the sounds in a word, chosen because this child already loves to line small things up. Another child would hear the sounds through claps, taps on the table, blocks snapped onto a row, or coins laid down one at a time. The parts worth keeping are the order — say the word, push one counter per sound, then write one letter per counter — with CVC words before digraphs, phonetic spellings welcomed as progress, and exception words kept separate and few. The goal is that he can hear a word as separate sounds and write a letter for each. What he pushes to feel them is yours to choose.
