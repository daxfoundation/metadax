# Quick maths facts with dominoes — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Hana · **Learner:** Milo · **Age band:** 7–9 · **Subject:** Math

## Where the learner was stuck

Milo, who is eight, can already count on to find an answer. Asked for 6 plus 7, he starts at 6 and counts seven more on his fingers, and he gets there — slowly, and the same slow way every time, even for sums he has met a hundred times. Nothing has become automatic. Each fact is rebuilt from one, as if for the first time. When anything is timed or rushed, the counting falls apart and he guesses. The problem is not accuracy when he has all the time in the world; it is that the facts are not yet at his fingertips, and the counting eats all his attention before he can get to the actual maths.

## The approach (built through what they love)

A domino shows a number as a fixed pattern of dots, not a row to be counted one at a time. Milo already reads the pips on a tile as a shape — he sees five without counting to five — and that is exactly the habit quick facts are built on. So every fact starts on a tile. Doubles are the double tiles: the double-six is 6 and 6. Doubles plus one is that same double with one more pip beside it: 6 and 6 is twelve, so 6 and 7 is one more, thirteen. Make ten is two tiles whose pips fill a ten-frame: a six needs a four to make ten, so 6 and 5 is one past ten, eleven. The work is five minutes a day, not one long weekly sit, because facts become automatic through short, frequent practice. The game steps up after a streak of correct answers and eases back after a miss, so it stays at the edge of what Milo can do without tipping into guessing.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

A session is short — five minutes is the whole thing, done most days rather than once a week. It opens with Milo in the lead: he sets up a topple run and teaches Hana his rule for how far apart the tiles have to stand so the whole line falls. This is real; Hana does not set them up as well as he does. Then one strategy at a time, named on an actual tile in Milo's hands: "that is a double, six and six, so you just know it is twelve." The guide's job is to listen for the difference between Milo recalling a fact and Milo quietly recounting under the table — the same answer arrives, but they are not the same thing, and only the recalled ones are the goal. Stop at five minutes even if a run is going well; coming back tomorrow matters more than pushing today.

## What only a person can do

Hana can hear the two-second pause and the moving lips that mean Milo is counting on in his head rather than remembering, and she can gently say the fact with him so the recall path gets used instead. She can tie a fact to a tile he toppled a minute ago, not a generic one. She can tell when a wrong answer is a tired guess rather than a real gap, and call it there for the day. And she decides when five minutes is up — stopping on a good one, so the next session starts from willingness rather than dread. A page cannot judge any of that.

## Where the learner leads

Milo leads the setup. He knows, better than Hana, how to space a long line of dominoes so none of them is left standing after the first push, and he teaches her his rule for it. That reading of the tiles as whole patterns — seeing the pips without counting them — is the very thing that makes maths facts quick, and it is already his. Hana's part is to name it: the thing you do when you glance at a tile and just know it is a five is the same thing we are building for six and six, and for six and four making ten. He supplies the skill; she points out that he already has it.

## The game on the learner's side

The game shows a fact as two domino tiles and asks for the total, one at a time. Three levels: level one is the doubles, read straight off the double tiles; level two is doubles plus one and make ten, with the tiles arranged so the strategy is visible; level three mixes the facts within twenty with the tiles faded, so Milo is leaning on recall. Difficulty steps up after three correct in a row and steps back down after a miss. Wrong answers name the specific slip — counted on from one instead of using the double, went one too far on a near-double, or did not use the ten — so Hana's note after can say exactly which strategy to practise next. Nothing bad happens on a wrong answer; it just tells the two of them what tomorrow's five minutes is for.

## When to seek an evaluation

Most children make their facts automatic with short daily practice over a few weeks or months, and needing to count on for a while is completely ordinary. But if, after a long stretch of patient five-minute sessions, a child still rebuilds every basic fact from one with nothing at all sticking, or if numbers and amounts seem to slip and rearrange in ways the child cannot notice or correct, or if the effort is sharp and persistent across many kinds of work and not just maths, it may be worth talking to someone whose work is understanding how children learn. A family doctor or an early-learning specialist can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. You do not need a label to have that conversation, and what you learn is yours to use however you choose.

## A way, never the way

Dominoes are one picture of quick facts, chosen because this child already loves them and already reads their dots as shapes. Another child would get there through finger patterns, a ten-frame, dice, or a number line they can picture. The parts worth keeping are the strategy-first order — doubles, then doubles plus one, then make ten — the five-minutes-a-day rhythm, and stopping while it is still going well. The goal is that the facts come without counting. What you hang them on is yours to choose.
