# Finding the earliest gap with stepping stones — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Grace · **Learner:** Wren · **Age band:** 7–9 · **Subject:** Other

## Where the learner was stuck

Wren, who is eight, came to learning at home after a hard year, described by the parent as "behind" in both reading and maths — but behind in a scattered, uneven way. Wren reads some words easily and stalls on others that look no harder; adds some small numbers instantly and is lost on others; seems fine one minute and at sea the next. Every curriculum Grace opened assumed a starting line, and Wren might be ahead of it in one place and well behind it in another. Start too high and Wren shuts down; start too low and Wren is bored and insulted. The real stuck point was not any single skill — it was that Grace had no way to tell where the first real gap was, and so no idea where to begin.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Wren loves crossing the creek behind the house on its line of stepping stones, and already knows the rule: you cross in order, you test each stone with a bit of weight before you trust it, and if one wobbles you steady yourself there — you do not leap for the far bank. That is exactly the shape of the check. It lays a short line of stones in the order skills are usually built: hearing and saying letter sounds, blending simple three-sound words like cat and sun, adding within ten, and reading a two-digit number as tens and ones — knowing that 24 is two tens and four ones. Grace walks Wren across gently, one stone at a time, and the first stone that wobbles is where the next real session will begin. The point is to find the earliest wobble, not the latest — to go back to the first loose stone, not to leap ahead to where the trouble shows most.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Say what this is before you start: "We are finding the comfy place to begin, not catching you out." Then cross the stones in order and keep it light. Ask each one plainly, give Wren time, and move to the next stone only while they are steady. Once two stones in a row wobble, stop asking harder ones — you have found the edge, and pushing past it only teaches Wren that the check is a test to fail. Note which stones were solid, which wobbled, and which were not there at all. Reading the results is simple: find the earliest wobble, and begin the next real session one stone before it, on the last solid stone, so the first lesson is a win Wren can feel. Keep the whole thing under ten minutes. A short, kind crossing tells you more than a long one that ends in tears.

## What only a person can do

Grace can tell a true gap from a nervous stumble — the wobble that means "I never learned this" from the one that means "I am tired, or shy, or testing whether this is safe." She can keep her face and voice easy so a check never curdles into an exam. She can notice when "I don't know" arrives because the session has simply run out of road for today, and stop there. She can hold the whole picture — that Wren was solid on letter sounds but wobbled on blending — in a way that turns four small observations into one clear place to start. And she decides, watching Wren, when enough has been found for one day. A page cannot read a child it has upset.

## Where the learner leads

Wren knows the real creek far better than Grace does — which stones are safe, which tip if you land wrong, how to test one with your weight before you trust it. Before the check, Wren leads: teaching Grace how to cross the real stones without getting a wet foot. And that method Wren is teaching — go in order, test gently, steady yourself at the first wobble rather than leaping — is precisely the method of the check itself. Grace's part is to point that out: the careful way Wren crosses the creek is the careful way the two of them will find where to start. The learner supplies the method; the guide names what it is for.

## The game on the learner's side

This one is built to be gentle, not to push, so it works differently from a game that races ahead. It shows one stone at a time: a letter sound to say, a short word like cat to blend, a sum within ten, a number to split into tens and ones. There is no timer and no score shown to Wren — stones simply light up as "crossed," and the mood is a walk across the creek, not a quiz. If a stone wobbles twice, the path pauses there kindly — "this is a good stone to practise; let's start here next time" — rather than marking anything wrong. The stones stay in their fixed, build-up order instead of jumping harder after a streak, because the whole job is to find the earliest gap, not to find the ceiling. The guide key quietly records which stone was the first to wobble, so Grace knows exactly where the next real session begins.

## When to seek an evaluation

Starting behind after a disrupted year is ordinary, and most gaps close once you begin at the right stone and build forward patiently. But some patterns are worth raising plainly with a professional. If letter sounds are not there at all even after lots of gentle exposure, if blending simple words does not come even with short daily practice over months, if the whole picture stays far behind what you would expect for the age and does not shift over a long stretch, or if you notice things like not seeming to hear the difference between similar sounds, squinting or headaches or losing the place when reading, or words that seem to move on the page — these are worth a conversation. Your family doctor, or an educational or reading evaluation through your province or state, is the right place to ask. None of these is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and you are not expected to work this out on your own. Asking early simply gives you more information and more options.

## A way, never the way

Stepping stones are one way to picture finding the first gap, chosen because this child already crosses a real creek this way. Another guide would picture the lowest loose rung on a ladder, the first wobbly block in a tower, or the earliest bar in a song where the fingers stumble. The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the creek: check in the order skills are built, find the earliest wobble rather than the latest, and begin one stone before it so the first lesson is a win. The goal is to find the first real gap kindly, and to start there. How you picture the crossing is yours to choose.
