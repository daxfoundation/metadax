# Letters on the move — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Lena · **Learner:** Kit · **Age band:** 4–6 · **Subject:** Reading

## Where the learner was stuck

Kit, who is five, never stops moving. Running, jumping, spinning — from the moment he wakes to the moment he falls asleep, he is on his feet and going. Lena had been following a structured reading programme, doing the lessons as they came: sitting at the table, looking at cards, filling in sheets. For a while it worked. Then it stopped. The lessons began to drag; Kit would wriggle away from the table before the page was done, and Lena found herself chasing him around the room and ending sessions in frustration on both sides. She wondered whether to abandon the programme and buy something else, but the programme was not the problem — the sitting was. Kit can hear a sound, copy it back, and recognise a letter he has met before. What he cannot do is sit still long enough for a lesson built for a child who can. The gap is not his attention and not his willingness — it is that the delivery is asking him to be something he is not.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Kit loves to run and jump and be on the move, so the letters come to him on the floor. Lena keeps the same programme and the same material — same letter sounds, same order — and changes only how they meet it. Letters chalked on the path or written on sticky notes taped to the floor become a track: Lena says a sound and Kit hops to the letter that makes it. At the fridge, a handful of magnetic letters are waiting; Lena says a word sound by sound and Kit builds it standing up, choosing the letter for each sound and snapping it into place. Writing happens big: the page is taped to the wall, or the chalk goes on the paving, or Kit writes the letter in the air first with his whole arm before anything touches paper. Every round is five minutes, and Lena stops while it is still fun — before Kit runs off, not after. The programme goes at exactly the same pace as before. The body is just along for the ride now.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Use what is already in the programme — the sounds introduced that week, the letters just met — and move the lesson off the table. Chalk six or eight letters on the path, or stick paper squares to the kitchen floor; say a sound and let Kit run to it, then say the next. Keep the track short to start — four letters — and add more as he gets quicker. At the fridge, say a word and let him build it one letter at a time while standing; three-letter words first, one magnet per sound. For writing, tape the page to the wall at shoulder height and let him write standing, or take it outside with chalk; air-writing with the full arm before pen-on-paper settles the shape into his body before it reaches his hand. Five minutes is the target — hold it in your head before you start, and stop when time is up even if there was room for one more. Everything you need is already on the shelf. Praise the finding, the moving, and the having-a-go, because the moving *is* the work here.

## What only a person can do

Lena can watch Kit reach toward the wrong letter and say the sound again softly before he lands on it, so the correction comes before the mistake rather than after — a page cannot do that. She can hear whether he is guessing the sound or truly hearing it, and add one slow repetition only when he needs it. She can move the taped letters closer together on a tired day and further apart when he has energy to burn. She can decide that one good word built at the fridge is enough, and end on the snap of the last magnet, so sessions stay something Kit runs back to. And she can tell the difference between Kit needing more movement and Kit needing rest — two different things that look alike from the outside. A printed lesson plan cannot adjust to the child in the room.

## Where the learner leads

Kit leads with his feet. The route he takes across the chalked letters, the order he reaches for the magnets, the size of the letters he draws in the air — these are his, and Lena follows. She sets out the letters; he chooses the path. She says the sound; he decides how fast to get there. When he adds a jump between letters or crawls to the fridge instead of walking, she lets him, because the sound he says when he arrives is what matters. His way of getting there is his own, and the learning rides on top of it.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is a short round the parent runs outdoors, on the kitchen floor, or at the fridge, with chalk or sticky notes and a handful of magnetic letters — nothing to plug in. Lena says a sound; Kit moves to the letter that makes it, builds a word at the fridge, or writes it big on the wall. Three levels set the reach: level one, four or five letters on the floor, one sound each, and Kit hops to the right one; level two, the letters build a three-sound CVC word at the fridge, one magnet per sound; level three, Kit writes the word on the wall or path, air-writing once first with his whole arm. It steps up after a run of sounds he found without hesitating and eases back after a miss, so the sounds always stay at the edge of what he knows. There is no losing: a wrong hop just means Lena says the sound once more, slowly, and Kit tries again. This is a game played with chalk and magnets and two people moving around; it is not a screen to switch on. It pairs well with [the spelling-by-sound-counters sample](spelling-by-sound-counters.md), where sounds are pushed into a line with counters before a letter is written, and with [the from-tracing-to-own-words sample](from-tracing-to-own-words.md), where writing grows from what the child can already manage. ## When to seek an evaluation

A very active child who cannot sit through a seated lesson is not usually a sign that anything is wrong — at five, moving while learning is often how the learning sticks, and for nearly every child this age, taking the lesson off the table is all it takes. But if, over many months of short, playful movement sessions, a child still cannot connect a spoken sound to its letter even when running to it, cannot build the simplest three-letter word one magnet at a time, grows sharply distressed whenever letters and sounds come up and not with other work, or the gap between what he can say and what he can do on the page keeps widening rather than closing, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how young children learn. A family doctor, or a reading or early-learning specialist, can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later only gives you more options.

## A way, never the way

Chalked letters on the path is one way to bring a moving child into reading, chosen because this child cannot sit still and did not need to. Another child would arrive through a different door: letters on cards shuffled and dealt across a table, sounds clapped while walking around the room, words built with foam blocks or pebbles on the ground. The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the chalk: take the programme's material and meet it with the body the child already has — say the sound, move to the letter, build the word standing, write it big. The goal is that the letter and the sound meet somewhere he can hold them, even if that somewhere is always moving. What he hops to, reaches for, or draws in the air is his to choose.
