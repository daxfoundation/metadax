# Science words that stick — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Yusuf · **Learner:** Omar · **Age band:** 10–12 · **Subject:** Science

## Where the learner was stuck

Omar, who is eleven, can learn a science word for Friday's quiz and get it right — then lose it clean by the following week. "Evaporation," "friction," "dissolve": he memorises the definition the night before, says it back, and a few days later looks blank at the same word. Yusuf used to think Omar was not trying, but he is: he is cramming definitions as strings of words with nothing underneath them, the way you might memorise a phone number you never dial. A word learned that way has no handle, so there is nothing to pick it back up by. The gap is not his memory for things that matter to him — he can tell you every part of a thing he built — it is that these words never got attached to anything he could see, touch, or do. A definition with nothing behind it slides straight off.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Omar is a maker: he would rather do a thing than be told about it, and he draws easily. So each new word is given something to hang on, in four small steps, and never more than three new words in a week. First, the word gets an **object or an action** — something he can hold or do. "Dissolve" is a spoon of sugar stirred into warm water until it vanishes; "friction" is rubbing his two hands together until they are warm; "evaporation" is a wet handprint on the counter watched until it is gone. Second, he writes **his own definition**, in his own plain words — not the textbook's. Third, he makes a quick **drawing** of it, however rough. Fourth, he uses the word in **a sentence about something he actually did that week**: not "friction is a force," but "I slowed my bike by braking, and friction is what the brakes used." Those four — object, own words, picture, real sentence — go on a card, and the card joins a **running word wall** on the kitchen door. Each day, for **two minutes**, they glance over the wall: he reads a word, says what it means in his words, and that is all. At most three new words a week, so the wall grows slowly and nothing is ever crammed.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Cap it at three new words a week, and defend that cap — a full wall of half-learned words is worse than three that stick. For each word, insist on all four handles, in order: hold or do the thing first, because the object is what the word will hang on; then his *own* definition, and gently refuse the textbook wording even when it is more "correct," because a word in his words is a word he owns; then a drawing, rough is fine; then a sentence tied to something he really did this week, which is where the word stops being abstract. Keep the daily review to a true two minutes — read a word, say it back, move on — because it is the coming-back, not the length, that fixes a word in place. Review old words more than new ones; the wall is there so nothing quietly falls off it. When a word has survived a couple of weeks of two-minute glances, let him retire it to a "known" list and feel the wall turn over. Praise the drawing and the real-life sentence, not the matching of a definition. Stop on a word he said back easily.

## What only a person can do

Yusuf can find the object or action that makes a particular word click for *this* child — that warm-hands moment for friction — rather than the generic example a book prints for everyone. He can hear the difference between Omar reciting a definition and Omar understanding it, the same words in the air meaning two different things, and only a listener beside him can tell which. He can hold the line at three words a week when Omar, or a worksheet, wants to pile on ten. He can tie a word to something Omar actually did on Tuesday, because he was there on Tuesday. And he can decide a word has stuck and retire it, or that it is wobbling and send it back for another week — a judgement no quiz score, taken on one anxious Friday, can make. A printed glossary cannot rub its hands together or remember what the child did this week.

## Where the learner leads

Omar leads with his hands and his own words. The definition that will last is the one he phrases himself, so the session hands him that job rather than a sentence to copy. His drawing leads too — it is his picture of the thing, and a word pinned to his own picture has somewhere to live. Yusuf's part is to name what is happening: the thing you just did, and the way you just said it, that is the word — we are only giving it a label to carry around. He supplies the doing, the wording, and the picture; Yusuf supplies the handle and keeps the wall honest. When Omar wants to add a word of his own, something he met while making something, that goes straight on the wall — a word he came to himself is already halfway stuck.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is the word wall itself, run by the family on a door or a wall with cards, a pen, and whatever small thing shows the word. Making a card is the first round: do or hold the thing, write your own definition, draw it, use it in a sentence about your real week. The daily round is two minutes: read a word off the wall and say back what it means in your own words. Three levels set the reach: level one, the guide supplies the object and the learner does the other three handles; level two, the learner finds his own object or action for the word; level three, he adds a word he met himself while making something and builds its whole card. It steps up after a run of words that survive the two-minute review and eases back if words start slipping, which is the signal to add fewer and review more. There is no losing: a word that slips just goes back to the "still learning" side of the wall for another week. This is a wall a family keeps by hand; it is not a screen to switch on. It runs well alongside [the one-question sample](one-question-a-week.md), where the words from each week's answer are exactly the ones worth putting on the wall.

## When to seek an evaluation

Learning a word for a quiz and losing it soon after is extremely common — it is what cramming does, to all of us — and for nearly every child, tying words to real objects and reviewing them two minutes a day is enough to make them hold, which is the whole point of this one. But if, over many months of this gentle, object-first practice, a learner cannot hold on to even a single new word tied to something he touched and did, cannot say back in his own words a term he used correctly a day before, loses words as fast as they are added no matter how few are introduced, or grows sharply distressed whenever words and their meanings are involved and not with other work, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how children learn. A family doctor, or a learning or language specialist, can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later only gives you more options.

## A way, never the way

A word wall with four handles per word is one way to make science vocabulary stick, chosen because this child makes and draws and learns by doing. Another child would get there through a different door: a word acted out in a charade, a term taught to a younger sibling, a card shuffled into a game, a word spoken into a running list. The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the door on the kitchen: give each word an object or action, let the learner define it in his own words and draw it, tie it to something he really did that week, review it two minutes a day, and add no more than three new words at a time. The goal is that a word learned is a word kept, because it has something real to hang on. What you hang it on is yours to choose.
