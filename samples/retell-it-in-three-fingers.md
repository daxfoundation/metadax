# Retelling it in three fingers — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Owen · **Learner:** Lily · **Age band:** 7–9 · **Subject:** Reading

## Where the learner was stuck

Lily, who is eight, listens to a whole story read aloud and then, asked what it was about, says "I don't remember" — or gives one short line that catches a stray detail and misses the point, like "there was a dog" after a story in which the dog barely mattered. It is not that she was not listening; it is that the story arrives as a stream and nothing holds it together afterwards, so when it stops there is nothing to reach back for. Pages of comprehension questions had not helped, because they ask her to find what she never gathered in the first place. The gap is not attention and not her memory for things she cares about — it is that she has no handle to pick a story up by once it is over.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Lily loves to draw, and she will tell you everything happening in one of her pictures before she can tell you a thing about a page of words. So the retell is built on pictures and on her own hand. First the chunks are kept short: Owen reads one or two paragraphs, stops, and they retell that much before going on, instead of waiting for the end of a long story once it has all run together — the chunks lengthen only as retelling the short ones gets easy. Then comes the handle: three fingers. One finger for who, one for what happened, one for how it ended; she says the three answers in a row, and those three said together are the summary. For a story she is struggling to hold, she draws a quick three-box sketch first — who, what happened, how it ended, one rough picture each — and tells it from the boxes. While Owen reads, she is allowed to jot a single word now and then, so her hands stay in it. Five minutes, and they stop on a retell she nailed.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Keep the chunks short at first — one or two paragraphs, then stop and retell — because a long passage is only a stack of short ones, and she can hold a short one. Start with Lily leading: she shows Owen a picture she drew and tells him everything in it, which is retelling, done fluently, on her own ground. Then read a chunk and hold up three fingers: who? what happened? how did it end? Let her answer each, then have her say all three in a row so she hears that the three together are the whole thing. If she stalls, reach for the three-box sketch before reaching for more questions. Lengthen the chunks only when the short ones come easily. Celebrate the three-in-a-row retell out loud — that, not the number of pages, is the win — and stop while she still wants the next chunk.

## What only a person can do

Owen can hear the difference between Lily not remembering a story and Lily not knowing how to pick it back up — the same "I don't remember," two different problems, and only someone listening can tell which. He can choose where to stop reading so a chunk breaks at a natural seam rather than mid-thought. He can see when she needs to draw the three boxes before she can say them, and hand her the paper without making it a big deal. He can pick the next passage around something she drew that morning, so the story feels like hers. And he can decide that three retold calmly beats ten rushed, and end on a good one. A page cannot choose where to pause for the child in front of it.

## Where the learner leads

Lily retells better than anyone when the story is one of her own drawings — she will narrate every corner of a picture, who is there, what is happening, how it turns out, without being asked. That is the whole skill, already hers. Owen's job is to point it out: the way she tells what is happening in her picture is exactly what three fingers ask of a story someone else wrote — who, what happened, how it ended. She supplies the retelling; he names it and turns it onto a new story. On her side, too, the first thing she meets is an invitation to tell the story in her own picture before anyone asks her about a page.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is one short round a parent can run with nothing but their hands and, if it helps, a scrap of paper folded into three boxes. The grown-up reads a chunk aloud — one or two paragraphs — then holds up three fingers and asks for who, what happened, and how it ended, and Lily says the three in a row. Three levels set the length: level one is a single paragraph, level two is two paragraphs, level three is a short whole story told in parts. It steps up after a run of clean three-finger retells and eases back after one that misses, so the chunk stays as long as she can hold and no longer. A retell that catches a small detail but misses the point is not marked wrong; it is named — "that is a true thing from the story; now give me the who and the how it ended" — so she learns the difference between a detail and the point. The three-box sketch is always allowed, and so is a single jotted word while she listens.

## When to seek an evaluation

Losing the thread of something heard once, and needing a handle to pick a story back up, is ordinary — and for most children the three-finger retell, built on shorter chunks, is enough to turn it around, which is the point of this session. But if, over many months and across many kinds of material, a child cannot say back even a one- or two-paragraph story told slowly and in parts, loses a spoken story almost as soon as it is told, cannot hold who did what from the start of a tale to its end, or tires sharply whenever a task asks her to retell rather than recall a single fact, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how children learn. A family doctor, or a reading or language evaluation through your province or state, is the right place to ask. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later means more options.

## A way, never the way

Three fingers and a three-box sketch are one way to give a story a handle, chosen because this child thinks in pictures and tells them easily. Another child would hold a story with a comic strip, three toys stood in a row for beginning-middle-end, or simply the question "who wanted what, and did they get it?" The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the fingers: shorten the chunk until she can hold it, then retell who, what happened, and how it ended as three answers in a row, and lengthen only as it gets easy. For an older learner who reads every word fluently but still cannot say what a passage was about, the next step is [reading-for-meaning-animal-facts](reading-for-meaning-animal-facts.md). The goal is that she can say what happened. What she holds it with is hers to choose.
