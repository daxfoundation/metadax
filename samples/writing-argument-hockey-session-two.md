# Turning a hockey argument into a paragraph (session two) — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*An illustrative example made with a simulated family — not a real family's record. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Dana · **Learner:** Theo · **Age band:** 13–15 · **Subject:** Writing

## Where the learner was stuck

Theo, who is fourteen, can argue for ten minutes straight about which player belongs on a line — reasons, counter-examples, the lot — but asked to write a single argument paragraph, Theo stalls at the first sentence and writes nothing. Session one got the spoken case onto paper in fragments: a strong claim and one reason, but the reasons ran together and there was no line answering the other side. The next-time note the learner page left at the end of session one said, in Theo's words: "the start is fine now, but the middle is a pile — and I don't know how to say the other guy is wrong without just saying he's wrong."

## The approach (picked up from the next-time note)

This session keeps what landed and moves one step on the exact spot the note named. The claim sentence is left alone — it works now, so it is not re-taught. The whole session is the middle: taking the pile of reasons and giving it a shape. The move is to pick the two strongest reasons and give each its own sentence, then add one sentence that names what the other side would say and answers it — the thing the note asked for. Every example is a real hockey argument Theo already makes out loud, written down in that four-part shape: claim, reason one, reason two, the answer to the other side.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Open with a callback, not a reset: Dana reads back Theo's claim sentence from session one and says it is staying because it works. Then the whole session is the middle. Theo argues a hockey take out loud first, Dana catches the two strongest reasons and reflects them back, and Theo writes each as its own sentence. The hardest sentence — answering the other side — comes last, modelled once on a take Theo does not hold, then written on one he does. Pace it to how session one ended: the note said Theo tired quickly once it felt like an English class, so keep it short, keep it hockey, and stop while it is still going well.

## What only a person can do

Dana can hear which two of Theo's five spoken reasons are actually the strongest and say so — a page cannot weigh an argument it has never heard. Dana can tell when Theo's "this is dumb" means the task is too long rather than too hard, and cut it there. She can catch the moment Theo argues brilliantly out loud and freeze it — "say that again, that exact sentence, now write that" — turning speech into the written line before it evaporates. And she can decide to answer the other side together, out loud, before Theo has to write it alone.

## Where the learner leads

Theo knows hockey far better than Dana, and that is the engine of the session. Before any writing, Theo leads: he lays out the argument in full, and Dana genuinely learns something about the game she did not know. The hardest written sentence — the one answering the other side — works because Theo already does it in speech without noticing; Dana's job is to point out that Theo just answered an opponent out loud, and that the paragraph needs exactly that sentence. The learner supplies the argument; the guide names the shape.

## The game on the learner's side

The game builds one paragraph in four moves, each a short choice rather than a blank page. First it offers three claim sentences and asks which states a position rather than a fact. Then it gives a jumble of reasons and asks Theo to pick the two strongest and drop the rest — the step the note asked for. Then it shows four sentences and asks which one answers the other side instead of just repeating the claim louder. It steps up as Theo gets a run right and eases back after a miss, and every wrong choice names the slip — "that is a fact, not a position," or "that repeats your claim, it does not answer theirs" — so Dana's next-time note can say exactly where to pick up.

## When to seek an evaluation

Plenty of people argue far better than they write, and a gap between a confident speaker and a stalled writer is ordinary, especially at this age. But if, over many sessions and many subjects, getting any words onto the page stays far harder than it should given how fluently the learner speaks — if the hand freezes in a way that does not ease with practice, or written output stays sharply behind spoken ability across everything, not just writing — it may be worth asking someone whose work is how young people learn. A family doctor or a learning specialist can observe and tell you what they see. You do not need a label to ask, and what you learn is yours to use as you choose.

## A way, never the way

Hockey is the way in here only because it is the thing Theo argues about without being asked. Another learner would turn a paragraph through a video game, a court case on a show, or a fight about a band. And session two went to the middle only because that is where this learner's note pointed; another note would send the next session somewhere else entirely. The shape — claim, reasons, an answer to the other side — is worth keeping; the subject it is built through is yours to choose, and the note tells you where to go next.
