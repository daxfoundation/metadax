# Counting with what she already plays with — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Nadia · **Learner:** Posy · **Age band:** 4–6 · **Subject:** Math

## Where the learner was stuck

Posy, who is four, can say "one, two, three, four, five" all the way to ten, and she says it proudly. But put four toy cars in front of her and ask how many, and she points and recites the number song — sometimes touching the same car twice, sometimes running the words faster than her finger — and lands on a number that has nothing to do with four. Ask her again and she gives a different answer. Nadia had wondered whether to buy a workbook, but the trouble is not that Posy needs more pages. The counting words have arrived as a tune, the way a nursery rhyme does, before they have been hooked onto actual things. The gap is not her memory and not her effort — it is that she does not yet know that counting means one word for one thing, and that the last word you say is the answer to "how many?"

## The approach (built through what they love)

Posy lines things up all day — her toy cars nose to tail along the skirting board, pasta shapes sorted into little rows, blocks stacked and counted off. That lining-up is already the exact move counting needs, so Nadia builds on it rather than bringing in anything new. They start with a small pile of the real things Posy plays with. Posy touches one and *moves* it to a new spot as she says the number — touch and move, one car at a time, so a counted car physically leaves the pile and cannot be counted twice. When the pile is gone, Nadia asks "so how many?" and, at first, answers it herself: "four — the last number you said is how many there are." After a few days Posy says the last number back. Then comes "one more": add a car, and instead of recounting from one, "we had four, one more, so…" — the first taste of counting on. Last, with just three or four things on a plate, Nadia covers and uncovers them for a moment and asks how many *without* counting — small groups the eye can simply see. Five minutes, and they stop on a pile she got. No worksheets anywhere: the maths is in her own hands moving her own toys.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Keep it to five minutes, most days, with whatever Posy is already playing with — cars, pasta, blocks — never a special "maths" object. Begin with the move that makes counting honest: touch one thing and slide it away as you say its number, so each thing is counted once and only once. If she double-counts or outruns her finger, slow her hand, not her voice. When the pile is counted, always ask "how many?" and, for the first while, answer it yourself so she hears that the last number *is* the total; hand that question to her only once she is ready. Keep the piles tiny — three, four, five — because number sense is built on small amounts seen clearly, not large ones rushed. Bring in "one more" as its own small game: add one, and nudge her to say the next number rather than starting over. Save subitising — knowing three at a glance — for a plate of two, three, or four, uncovered for a second. Praise the careful one-at-a-time touching, out loud, because that care *is* the skill. Stop on a pile she counted right, even if there was time for one more.

## What only a person can do

Nadia can watch Posy's finger and her mouth at the same time and see the exact place they come apart — the finger on the third car while the mouth is already saying "five" — and gently bring them back together, which no page of printed dots can do. She can tell the difference between Posy not knowing the number words and Posy knowing them but not yet hooking them to things, two very different situations that look identical from across the room. She can decide the pile is one car too big today and quietly take one away. She can answer "how many?" for Posy for exactly as many days as Posy needs, and not one day longer. And she can read the moment the attention goes and stop on a win, so counting stays a thing Posy wants to come back to. A worksheet cannot move a car, follow a finger, or choose when to stop.

## Where the learner leads

Posy leads with her lining-up. Nobody taught her to set her cars nose to tail in a careful row — that is already hers, and it is the very heart of counting: things in a line, dealt with one at a time, in order. Nadia's part is to name what it is for. The careful way Posy lays her cars down is the careful way the two of them will count them: one car, one word, the next car, the next word. She supplies the habit of order; Nadia supplies the words that ride along on top of it. When Posy wants to count her own things, in her own line, her way, that is not a detour from the lesson — it *is* the lesson, and Nadia follows her lead.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is one short round a parent runs on the floor or at the table with a handful of whatever the child lines up — cars, pasta shapes, blocks, buttons. Deal out a small pile; the child touches and moves each thing to a new spot as she counts, then answers "how many?" with the last number she said. Three levels set the reach: level one is counting a pile of up to four, touch-and-move, with the grown-up saying the total; level two has the child say the total herself and play "one more" — add one, count on instead of starting over; level three covers a group of two, three, or four for a second and asks how many without counting at all. It steps up after a run of piles counted right and eases back the moment a pile gets muddled, so the amounts stay small and seeable. There is no losing: a miscount just means the grown-up and child slide the things back into the pile and count again, slower. This is a game played by hand with real toys; it is not a screen to switch on. It pairs well with the catching game in [the mistakes-are-safe sample](mistakes-are-safe-catch-my-mistake.md), where a grown-up miscounts on purpose for the child to catch.

## When to seek an evaluation

Reciting the number words long before they attach to actual things is an ordinary, healthy stage — for almost every four-year-old, a few weeks of exactly this kind of short, playful counting is all it takes for "how many?" to click. But if, over many months of gentle five-minute sessions, a child still cannot count a pile of three or four things one at a time without doubling back, cannot tell you that the last number said is the total even after you have shown her again and again, cannot hold the smallest amount in mind from one moment to the next, or grows sharply upset whenever counting comes up and not with other play, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how young children learn. A family doctor, or an early-learning specialist, can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later only gives you more options.

## A way, never the way

Counting her own toy cars in a line is one way into number sense, chosen because this child already lines small things up for the pleasure of it. Another child would get there through a different door: steps climbed and counted out loud, grapes eaten one at a time off a plate, spoons laid out for dinner, stones dropped into a tin. The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the cars: touch and move one thing per number so nothing is counted twice, answer "how many?" with the last number said, add "one more" to begin counting on, and let the eye learn to see two, three, and four at a glance. The goal is that she knows counting means one word for one thing and that the last word is the answer. What she counts to get there is hers to choose.
