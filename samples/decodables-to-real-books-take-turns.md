# Decodables to real books: take turns — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Tomas · **Learner:** Cass · **Age band:** 7–9 · **Subject:** Reading

## Where the learner was stuck

Cass reads his decodable readers in lessons without trouble — he sounds the words, he gets through the little book, he is accurate. But hand him a real book, the kind with a story in it, and ask him to read it on his own, and he closes it, or pushes it back, or asks to be read to instead. It is not that he cannot read the words; it is that a real book looks like a wall of them, with no promise that each one will be sound-out-able the way the decodables are, and the risk of hitting a word he cannot get feels too big to start. The decodables are safe because they only use what he has been taught; a real book makes no such promise, and so he will not step onto it alone. The gap is not his decoding — it is confidence, and the size of the leap between a controlled reader and an open book.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Cass loves being read to, and he loves animals — he will happily read a page out loud to the family cat when nobody is making him. So the bridge is built out of sharing and out of a soft audience. First, take turns: Tomas reads a page, Cass reads a line, and that is all that is asked of him to begin with. Over days, Cass's share grows — a line, then two, then a short paragraph — but only as fast as he stays willing, so the book never tips back into a wall. Second, the real books they choose are a notch easier than his lesson level, so that the words he meets are nearly all ones he can already get; a real book he can almost coast through does more for courage than a hard one does for skill. Third, when he reads his part, he reads it to the cat — or a stuffed animal, or his little sister — because reading to someone who cannot judge him takes the pressure clean off. And Tomas keeps his own read-alouds separate, at another time of day, purely for the pleasure of a story, so that being read to never turns into a turn Cass has to take. Ten minutes, and they stop on a page that went well.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Start with the turns clearly small: you read a page, he reads a line. Say so up front, so he knows exactly how little is being asked before he opens the book. Choose a real book a notch below his lesson level — easy enough that he meets almost only words he can already read. Grow his share by one line at a time, and only after a run of easy turns; the moment he tenses, shrink it back. Let him read his part to a pet or a toy, and sit a little to the side rather than leaning in, so it is reading-to-the-cat and not reading-to-be-checked. When he hits a word he cannot get, supply it quickly and move on — no stopping to sound it out mid-story, because the story's flow is what is being protected. Keep your own bedtime or sofa read-alouds entirely separate and pressure-free, so the pleasure of being read to stays pure and never becomes a lesson. Stop while he still has appetite, on a page that felt good.

## What only a person can do

Tomas can feel the exact moment Cass's willingness is about to tip and shrink the turn before the book becomes a wall again. He can choose the real book — judging it a notch easy, picking a story about the animals Cass loves — in a way no reading level alone could. He can supply a stuck word in half a second so the story never stalls, and know when to supply it and when to wait. He can keep two things apart that matter: the shared take-turns reading, and the pure read-aloud that asks nothing of Cass at all. And he can tell the difference between "this book is too hard" and "I am just nervous to start," and answer each rightly. A levelled book list cannot watch a child's shoulders and know when to make his turn smaller.

## Where the learner leads

Cass leads when he reads to the cat. Nobody has to push him; he picks a page and reads it aloud to an animal that will never correct him, and in doing it he is doing the whole thing — reading real words, off a real page, out loud, for the pleasure of it. That is the skill, already his, in the one setting where the fear is switched off. Tomas's part is to notice it and gently widen the circle: from the cat, to the cat-with-Tomas-nearby, to taking turns with Tomas on a book they share. Cass supplies the willing reading; Tomas keeps the audience soft and the turns small enough that the willingness survives being seen. The pace is set by the child, one line at a time.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is take-turns reading, one short round a parent runs with a real book a notch easier than lesson level and a soft audience — the cat, a stuffed animal, a patient sibling. The grown-up reads a page; the child reads his share to the animal. Three levels set the child's share: level one is one line per page; level two is two or three lines; level three is a whole short paragraph, or alternating pages. It steps up after a run of pages the child read happily and eases straight back after one that felt like too much, so his turn always sits just inside what he can do without strain. There is no losing: a word he cannot get is simply supplied by the grown-up and the story rolls on, because keeping the story moving matters more than winning any single word. The read-to-a-pet audience stays throughout, because the whole game rests on reading to someone who cannot judge. This is a game played with a paper book and a willing listener; it is not a screen that reads along. The step before this one — getting separate sounds to blend into whole words at all — is built out in [reading-blending-dinosaurs](reading-blending-dinosaurs.md).

## When to seek an evaluation

Hanging back from real books while reading decodables fine is common and usually about confidence, not ability — and for most children, small shared turns on easy, loved books are enough to carry them across, which is the point of this session. But if, over many months of gentle take-turns reading on books well within his decoding, a child still cannot read even a single easy line of unfamiliar text on his own, loses words he clearly knows the instant they appear outside a controlled reader, shows real and lasting distress at the sight of any book that is not a decodable, or tires sharply whenever reading is asked of him and not with other work, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how children learn to read. A family doctor, or a reading specialist through your province or state, can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later only gives you more options.

## A way, never the way

Taking turns and reading to the cat is one way across the gap from decodables to real books, chosen because this child loves being read to and loves animals. Another child would cross it differently: a comic where the pictures carry half the load, a book he has heard read aloud so many times he half-knows it, or reading a line each to a friend on a video call. The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the cat: take turns and grow the child's share slowly, pick real books a notch below lesson level, let him read to an audience that cannot judge, supply stuck words fast to protect the story, and keep your own read-alouds separate so they stay pure fun. The goal is that he will open a real book and read a little of it himself. What the book is about, and who he reads it to, is his to choose.
