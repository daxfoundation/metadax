# Blending sounds with dinosaurs — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Nora · **Learner:** Dash · **Age band:** 7–9 · **Subject:** Reading

## Where the learner was stuck

Dash could say every letter sound on its own without any trouble. The problem was that the sounds would not join up. Sounding out s-u-n out loud, Dash would then say a completely different word that started with s. Each sound stayed separate, like beads that would not string together into a word. Worksheets and flashcards had not shifted this. The frustration showed up quickly: after about two misses in a row, Dash would cover the page and stop.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Dinosaur names are built from the same phonics chunks that appear in ordinary words, and Dash already knew those names. That meant every blending exercise could feel like a dinosaur game rather than a reading drill. Short names came first — three sounds in rex, four in tops — and the game built up to five-chunk names like Velociraptor. Because Dash wanted to get the dinosaur names right, there was a reason to push the sounds together. The game also stepped up in difficulty after three correct answers in a row and stepped down after a miss, so the difficulty stayed close to the edge of what Dash could do.

## For the guide: the shape of a session

A session starts with Dash in the lead: Dash teaches Nora something about how to tell two dinosaurs apart. This is not a warm-up exercise — it is a real moment where Dash is the expert. Only after that does the blending work begin. The guide's steps are short: introduce one name, model the blending (say the sounds slowly, then faster, until the word pops out), then let Dash try. The game records which blends break down most often so the next session can target exactly those. Sessions are kept short, stopping before frustration rises too high. If Dash covers the page, the guide moves immediately to doing the sounds together rather than waiting or asking why.

## What only a person can do

Nora can see Dash's face change a moment before the page gets covered, and can move in before the frustration lands. Nora can also connect a specific dinosaur name that Dash mentioned to the sounds in that session, making the example feel personal rather than generic. Deciding when to stop — even if the game is not finished — is something only Nora can judge. A short successful moment at the end of a session matters more than getting through all the questions.

## Where the learner leads

At the start of every session, Dash teaches Nora one thing about dinosaurs: how to tell a Stegosaurus from an Ankylosaurus, or what made a Triceratops different from other ceratopsians. Nora listens and asks questions. This is not decoration — it signals that being the expert matters, and it lets the session start from a place of confidence. The same pattern appears on Dash's side of the page: the first thing Dash sees is an invitation to teach, not a question to answer.

## The game on the learner's side

The game shows letter sounds one at a time, using chunks from real dinosaur names. Dash picks which name the sounds blend into. Wrong answers are tied to the specific mistake behind them: for example, picking a word that starts with the first sound (guessing from the beginning rather than blending all the sounds) gives a different response than saying the sounds out loud but not joining them into a word. The game has three levels. Level one uses short three- and four-sound names like rex and raptor. Level two uses longer chunks like stego and bronto. Level three uses full names across four or five chunks. Difficulty steps up after three correct answers in a row and down after one miss, so it stays close to the edge of what Dash can do.

One part of the game is a spoken-sound item. The page prints a short line for Nora to read aloud — "Say the sound at the start of *stego*" — and the device itself says nothing. Dash hears the sound and taps the matching chunk among three or four choices on the screen, or circles it on the printed page. The matching chunk and one line of why live in the guide key, never on the learner's page, and the same step-up-after-three, step-down-after-one pacing applies. It is additive: a package that uses only the blending game is just as valid.

## When to seek an evaluation

If a learner can say each sound correctly but the sounds still do not join into words after several sessions — even with short bursts, patient modelling, and a topic they love — it is worth describing that to a professional and asking whether an evaluation would help. Other things worth noting: mixing up the order of sounds inside a word, frequently forgetting which sound goes with which letter even after practice, or getting much more tired from reading tasks than from other kinds of learning. When asking, describe what you see: say that the learner can produce each sound on its own but the sounds do not connect into words, and that the pattern has continued across multiple sessions. You do not need a label before asking for an evaluation. Getting one earlier rather than later means more options.

## A way, never the way

This page shows one approach that worked as a starting point for one child at one moment. A different child who loves something else entirely would need a different anchor. A child who connects to the sounds but freezes under any game pressure would need a different shape. The blending steps here — model slowly, speed up, let the word emerge — are grounded in how sounds work, but the way they are delivered, the pacing, and when to stop are all things only the guide in the room can judge. Use what fits. Leave the rest.
