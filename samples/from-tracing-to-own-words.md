# From tracing to her own words — a Meta DAX homeschool sample

*A made-up example built from a parent's description. A way, never the way.*

**Guide:** Ellen · **Learner:** Wren · **Age band:** 4–6 · **Subject:** Writing

## Where the learner was stuck

Wren, who is in kindergarten, traces beautifully. She follows the dotted letters, she copies a short sentence underneath a model, and the page comes out neat. Ellen is not worried that Wren cannot do it — she can — but she has started to wonder whether tracing is actually writing, or whether it is handwriting practice that stops short of the thing itself. Wren can form the letters; what she has not yet done is put her own words onto a page and watch them become writing. The gap, if it is one, is not skill and not effort — it is that tracing gives her someone else's words to follow, and nobody has yet shown her that the words can be hers. This sample is less about a stuck child than about the next small step past a step done well.

## The approach (built through what they love)

Wren will tell you everything about her day if you let her, and she draws what she did before she has the words for it. So the writing starts from her mouth and her pictures, not from a worksheet. Each day she says one sentence about her day — "We found a snail by the step" — and Ellen writes it down, saying each word as she writes it so Wren sees talk turn into marks. Then Wren copies just a word or two of her own sentence; not the whole line, only a piece she can finish while still fresh. The other half is labelling her own drawings: Wren draws a dog and writes the sounds she hears for it, and "dg" counts as a win, because she has listened to a word and matched letters to its sounds — which is the real engine under spelling. Nobody fixes "dg" to "dog" today. Five or ten minutes, and they stop on a word or a label she is pleased with. Hearing and writing the sounds in a word is the very next thing this leads to, built out fully in [spelling-by-sound-counters](spelling-by-sound-counters.md).

## For the guide: the shape of a session

Keep it short — five to ten minutes — and let Wren's talk start it. Ask for one sentence about her day and write it down for her, speaking each word as you write so she watches speech become print. Then have her copy only a word or two, not the whole sentence, so copying ends while she is still interested. On other days, or the same day, let her draw first and label the drawing with the sounds she hears; welcome "dg" for dog and "sn" for snail out loud as real writing, and resist tidying them into correct spelling — that comes later and not here. Praise the having-a-go at the sounds, specifically. Keep her own words at the centre: the sentence is hers, the drawing is hers, the labels are hers, and your job is to catch them on paper and hand them back. Stop on something she made, not when a line is "finished."

## What only a person can do

Ellen can take the sentence Wren actually says and write it down as she said it, so the words on the page are genuinely the child's — a worksheet can only offer words chosen in advance by a stranger. She can hear that "dg" means Wren caught the first and last sounds of dog and tell her that is exactly right, rather than marking it wrong. She can judge how much of a sentence Wren can copy before interest fades, and stop one word short of that. She can decide that today's win is one proud label and end there. And she can hold the quiet distinction at the heart of this: tracing builds the hand, but only a person can invite the words that make it writing. A page of dotted letters cannot ask Wren what she did today.

## Where the learner leads

Wren leads with her voice and her pictures. The sentences are hers before any pencil moves; the drawings carry the meaning that the letters will later spell out. That is the whole of early writing, already in her — she has things to say and ways to show them. Ellen's part is to name it: the story you just told me, and the picture you just drew, those are writing, we are only catching them on the page. She supplies the words and the pictures; Ellen supplies the handle that turns them into marks Wren can make herself. Over time the writing-down shifts from Ellen's hand to Wren's, a word at a time, as the child's own sentences pull her pencil along.

## The game on the learner's side

The game is one short round a parent runs at the table with paper, a pencil, and whatever Wren drew that day. First, "one-sentence news": Wren says a sentence about her day, the grown-up writes it, and Wren copies a piece of it. Then, "label your picture": Wren writes the sounds she hears for the things in her drawing. Three levels set the reach: level one is copying a single word of her own sentence and writing a first sound or two on a drawing; level two is copying two words and labelling with beginning-and-end sounds, like "dg"; level three is Wren having a go at a whole short word's sounds and copying a short phrase. It steps up after a run of happy, willing goes and eases back the moment it feels like a chore, because willingness is the thing being protected. There is no wrong answer here: a sound-spelling is a success by design, and a copied word that wobbles is still her own word on the page. This is a game played by hand with a pencil; it is not an app to open.

## When to seek an evaluation

Moving from tracing to a child's own words happens gradually, and sound-spellings like "dg" for dog are a normal, healthy stage on the road to standard writing — not a sign that anything is wrong, and for nearly every child this gentle kind of daily invitation is all it takes. But if, over many months of short, low-pressure sessions, a child cannot hear or write even the first sound of a spoken word, cannot copy a short familiar word she has just watched being written, shows real distress whenever a pencil and her own words are involved and not with other play, or seems unable to connect the marks on a page to the words she says aloud, it may be worth describing what you see to someone whose work is how children learn. A family doctor, or an early-learning or literacy specialist, can watch, ask questions, and tell you what they see. None of this is a diagnosis, you do not need a label before asking, and asking earlier rather than later only gives you more options.

## A way, never the way

Dictating a sentence and labelling a drawing is one way to carry a child past tracing into her own words, chosen because this child talks and draws readily. Another child would get there through a different door: telling a story into a toy microphone for you to write, making a label for every toy in a box, or captioning a photo from the day. The parts worth keeping are the moves, not the snail or the dog: let the child's own sentence be the text, write it down for her and let her copy a piece, welcome sound-spellings as real progress, and keep each session short and willing. The goal is that she sees her own words become writing and starts putting the sounds she hears onto the page. What she says, and what she draws, is hers to choose.
