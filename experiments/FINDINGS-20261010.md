# Findings — 10 October 2026

*Two questions about how a learning companion should use the commons: should it
fetch the right piece instead of a whole course, and should it think harder only
when it needs to?*

## What we tested, and why

A good learning companion should reach into the shared commons and pull back the one
piece a learner needs for this turn — the matching spot and just enough around it —
rather than loading an entire course into its working memory on every question. It
should also know when a question is easy enough for a quick answer and when it is
worth thinking harder. We ran two experiments to probe this. **X14** asks whether
fetching the spot (the matching node, its ancestors, and a small budgeted sample of
near-duplicate variants) holds answer quality while keeping the per-turn cost from
growing with the size of the library. **X13** and its follow-up **X13b** ask whether
a small fast model can answer by default while a cheap signal escalates to a larger,
slower model only when the extra thinking is actually needed. Throughout, we keep the
labels **MEASURED**, **ESTIMATED**, and **SIMULATED** on every number so a reader
knows what was observed versus modelled.

---

## X14 — Fetch the spot, not the course

Each learner question was answered two ways and then scored blind, out of 5, by a
separate grader (pass = score ≥ 4). **Arm A** put the whole course into context.
**Arm B** looked the spot up in a lightweight index, then sent only the matching node,
its ancestor trail (at most five entries), and up to three near-duplicate variants
sampled under a fixed 2,000-token budget. We ran this at three library sizes — small
(S, real content only), medium (M, about ten times larger), and large (L, about fifty
times larger) — to see whether fetching the spot still works as the commons grows.

### Quality against per-turn context cost — quality MEASURED, tokens ESTIMATED

| Size | Arm | Pass rate | Avg score (0–5) | Context tokens/turn, avg / max (est.) |
|------|-----|-----------|-----------------|---------------------------------------|
| S | A whole-course | 5/6 = 83% | 4.50 | 1,859 / 2,027 |
| S | B spot+sample | 5/6 = 83% | 4.33 | **592 / 1,309** |
| M | A whole-course | 5/6 = 83% | 4.67 | 22,879 / 24,946 |
| M | B spot+sample | 5/6 = 83% | 4.50 | **1,560 / 1,947** |
| L | A whole-course | 5/6 = 83% | 4.67 † | 118,236 / 128,918 |
| L | B spot+sample | 5/6 = 83% | 4.33 | **1,579 / 1,767** |

Fetching the spot matched whole-course quality in every cell — same pass rate at every
size, average score within 0.1–0.3 of a point.

**Token scaling.** The whole-course baseline grows about 64× from S to L and keeps
climbing with the library. The spot-and-sample cost rises once (S→M, as variants
appear to sample) and then flattens (M→L: 1,560 → 1,579 est. tokens/turn), pinned by
the budget. At the large size the spot-and-sample arm used roughly **75× fewer**
context tokens per turn than the whole-course arm. The index also kept finding the
right node as the library grew: its hit rate was flat at **0.87** across S, M, and L.
The point is that per-turn cost tracks the *question*, not the *library*.

### Caveats on X14

- **Only six questions were graded.** The blind eval drew six learner questions
  spanning a deep multi-level chain, a single-node course, a fraction example, and a
  data-handling node. Six is a small sample; read the quality numbers as directional.
- **Pseudo-translations.** The non-English variants are a deterministic, scripted
  pseudo-translation built as a deliberate worst case for word-overlap matching — they
  share almost no words with the English source. This is a **SIMULATED** stand-in, not
  real translated content.
- **The largest cells were capped (†).** For the large whole-course arm, the three
  biggest questions (the ~128K-token whole-course send) were **not** sent to a model;
  their scores were carried over from the measured medium library, where the
  whole-course context holds the same real content and only the token count grows. The
  smaller large-arm questions were run live.
- Token counts are an **ESTIMATED** `characters ÷ 4` approximation, not a real
  tokenizer, and the 10× / 50× libraries are **SIMULATED**, not a real large commons.

One more honest note: the one question that scored below a pass did so in *both* arms
(a thin example node that never cleanly defines its own topic) — that is a content
problem, not a retrieval one, so it does not separate the two arms.

## X13 and X13b — Fast then slow: the honest negative

The idea: answer the ordinary turn with **a small fast model**, and escalate to **a
large slow model from the same family** only when a cheap signal says the turn is hard
or high-stakes. If that works, the companion can run at scale without the larger model
on every turn. We followed a published fast/slow metacognition frame (arXiv:2110.01834)
for the escalation idea. The result is a negative one, and worth stating plainly.

**The small model matched the large one.** In X13, across 32 cases, the small model
passed every case the large model did (100% pass in all arms; mean blind score 4.94
small vs 4.91 large). Because the small model was never wrong, **every escalation was
wasted** — the governor's precision was 0. The ~51% cut in large-model tokens it showed
was just a by-product of escalating about half the turns, not of spending the larger
model where it changed the answer.

**X13b rebuilt the workload to be hard on purpose** — piloted and graded blind first,
keeping only the cases the small model genuinely failed — and still could not open much
of a gap. With a fully-scaffolded prompt the small model passed 57 of 60; with a
deliberately thin prompt *and* genuinely hard multi-step maths it still passed 55 of 60.
On the 40-case test set the arms landed at **90.0% (large) vs 87.5% (small)** — a
2.5-point gap. The small model **tied** the large one at 100% on the three "hard
capability" types (multi-step maths, diagnosing a misconception, long-context
retrieval). The only separation was in ambiguous-judgment and constraint-adherence.

**The escalation signals could not find the few real misses cheaply.** Against the five
real small-model failures, the best signal was the model's own stated confidence
(recall 1.00, precision 0.227) — but its confidence was barely calibrated: it averaged
0.928 when right and 0.910 when wrong, a difference of 0.018. Catching every miss meant
escalating **55% of turns**, which left the large-model token share at 61% (only a 39%
cut — **below** the ≥50% bar). A cheap self-check, where the small model reviews its own
answer, caught **0 of the 5** misses — an overconfident model rubber-stamps itself.

**On the one acute safety case, the large model was the one that got it wrong.** It
read a learner's "I don't want to be here anymore" as exam frustration and switched to
teaching the material; the small model handled it correctly. Across the safety type the gap
*reversed* — small model 100%, large model 85.7%. Escalating that turn to the larger
model made it worse. On this workload, the slower model was not the safer model.

All quality scores here are **MEASURED** (blind-graded). Costs and latency are
**ESTIMATED** only: tokens are a `characters ÷ 4` approximation, throughput is assumed,
the two tiers were run as same-subscription subagents rather than a metered endpoint,
and the repeat-failure counter was a **SIMULATED** field. The trustworthy parts are the
quality scores, the pass rates, and the signal precision/recall; absolute cost is not
claimed.

## What this changes in the design

We treat the following as **hypotheses for the next experiments**, not settled
conclusions:

1. **Small model by default.** On realistic companion tasks the small fast model was at
   or near parity with the large one, so it should carry the ordinary turn.
2. **A dedicated safety path that does not depend on either tier.** Safety handling was
   not reliably better on the larger model — it was worse on the one acute case — so the
   safe-escalation response should be its own path (a policy control), not something we
   hope a bigger model gets right.
3. **Spend the effort on retrieval quality.** Fetching the right spot, not a whole
   course, is where the measured win is. The questions that failed failed on content,
   not on model size.
4. **Escalate by situation type, not by confidence.** Self-reported confidence barely
   separated right from wrong. The only durable gap was in ambiguous-judgment and hard
   format/language constraints, so a cheap classifier for "this turn carries a hard
   constraint or is genuinely ambiguous" is a more promising route than a confidence
   threshold.

## Reproduce it

Each experiment is a single zero-dependency script (plain Node, no extra packages),
deterministic via a seeded pseudo-random generator and with no wall-clock or random
calls, so a rerun reproduces the same numbers.

- **X14** — engine `x14.cjs`. Run `node x14.cjs` for the metrics, and
  `node x14.cjs pack <dir>` to write the per-cell context packs used for the blind eval.
- **X13** — engine `x13.cjs`. Run `node x13.cjs cases`, then follow the pipeline:
  `cases` → `prompts` → run the fast and slow models → `govern` → run the escalated
  subset → `assemble-grade` → run the blind grader → `measures`.
- **X13b** — engine `x13b.cjs`. Run `node x13b.cjs cases`, then:
  `cases` → `pilot-prompts` → fast-model answers → `verify-prompts` → fast-model
  self-checks → `pilotgrade-prompts` → blind grade → `select` → `slow-prompts` →
  slow-model answers → `maingrade-prompts` → blind grade → `govern`.

**Run folders would need to be added to the public repo too** — they are not yet
published. The engines, their inputs, and all raw outputs live under these exact paths,
with every file under 2 MB:

| Path | Size |
|------|------|
| `experiments-runs/X14/`  | 484 KB |
| `experiments-runs/X13/`  | 376 KB |
| `experiments-runs/X13b/` | 496 KB |
| **total** | **~1.4 MB** |

Each folder holds the engine (`*.cjs`), the grader rubric, the prompts, the model
answers, the routing/grading decisions, and a `metrics.json` of every measure.

## Limits, and what we would test next

**X14.** Only six questions were graded; the larger libraries are synthetic; the
non-English variants are a scripted worst-case pseudo-translation; and the biggest
whole-course cells were capped and carried over. Collapsing near-duplicates into one
canonical node worked well on word overlap but was essentially blind to translations
(they share almost no words by construction) — so grouping a multilingual commons needs
a meaning-based layer, not word matching alone. Next: add a local meaning-based grouping
to recover translation matches, swap the synthetic large library for a real
multi-course corpus, and sweep the sample budget to find where quality starts to drop.

**X13 / X13b.** The governor test rests on only five real small-model failures, so it is
statistically underpowered; the honest headline is that the small model was at
near-parity, leaving little quality for any router to reclaim cheaply. Costs and latency
are estimated, not metered. Next: build a workload around the only axes that actually
separated the tiers — ambiguity and hard format/language constraints (strict word and
line limits, a non-English-first instruction, accessibility needs) — and test whether a
*targeted* classifier beats confidence; replace the self-check with an independent
two-sample-agreement probe and measure its recall; drive the two tiers through a real
metered endpoint so cost and latency are measured; and report the best-possible
("oracle") routing ceiling alongside every signal, since here even perfect routing
bought little over always-fast.
