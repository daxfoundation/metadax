# Intake — the questions to ask, for an expert teaching peers

You are a subject expert — a professor, a senior practitioner, a researcher —
leading other adults who are near your level: a seminar, a reading group, a study
circle of peers. This is a way to run intake, never the only way. Answer in plain
language, in your own words. About a dozen short questions. These learners are
adults and often experts themselves, so describe them by **where they sit in the
field and how they engage**, never a label or a diagnosis, and never by name. Ask
for nothing identifying — no real names, no institution, no email or phone. There
is no child here, so there is no child-safety framing.

## The session and the goal

- **Goal** — what do you want the group to come away able to do or discuss? In your
  words: "argue both sides of the replication debate", "read this proof and find
  the load-bearing step", "connect last week's paper to the open problem."
- **Who the peers are** — where do they sit in the field? Graduate students,
  fellow faculty, practitioners from a nearby discipline? Roughly how many?
- **Where the field sits** — what is settled, what is contested, what is open
  around this topic? This is what makes a peer session different from a lesson: it
  leans on debate, not drill.
- **How often you meet** — a single seminar, a reading series, a recurring circle.
  Pages say "session 1", never a date; the cadence sets what carries across
  sessions.
- **How it runs** — a seminar table, a shared screen, pre-reading then discussion,
  or a working session? This shapes what the page assumes.

## What the group is working through

- **The crux** — what is the hard or contested spot this session turns on? The more
  exact the better ("whether the assumption in §3 holds", "the jump from lemma 2 to
  the theorem").
- **A real sticking point** — a misreading or a point people reliably get snagged
  on, even experts. This tells you more than anything else.
- **Prior work** — what have the peers already read or done that this builds on?
  What is assumed background?
- **Source material** — the paper, the chapter, the proof, the dataset, the primary
  text this is grounded in. Paste or name it; the session is built around reading
  and arguing *this*, not a summary of it. Leave out anything identifying.
- **Where you expect disagreement** — the points where the group will likely split.
  These become the discussion pivots.

## Interests (these pick the theme)

- **What lands with this group** — for peers the "scene" skews to the field's own
  objects: a worked case, a timeline of how an idea developed, a data plot, two
  rival framings side by side. Name the kind that fits your discipline. Swapped if
  it stops landing.
- **Who can lead which part** — peers take turns being a step ahead; note who could
  open which thread. This becomes the "lead" moment, shared around the table.

## You, leading it

- **This exact topic for me** — at the frontier / solid / outside my main area.
  Even experts have edges; the page is written for where you are on *this* point.
- **Frame things for me** — terse and formal / build the argument / foreground the
  open questions / ground in examples.
- **Language** — English, French, both, or other.

If anything typed in looks like an email, phone number, link or an `@handle`, say
what it looks like and leave it out of everything you build.

## What you get back

A session package to lead the group through: a side for you with the pivots, the
contested spots, the source grounding, and what only the person at the table can
do; a peer side built around reading and arguing the source, with turns to lead. At
the end the page shows a short next-time note you copy to carry the thread into the
next session. Nothing leaves the device unless you copy it. MetaDAX is free.
